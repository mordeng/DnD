# NOVEL-CLAUDE.md -- Instructions for Writing Sessions

This file governs how Claude writes chapters for the light novel. Read this before every writing session.

---

## Voice: Third Person Omniscient Narrator

The novel uses a **Pratchett-style omniscient narrator** -- a distinct voice that knows everything, sees everything, and is deeply fond of these idiots while being fully aware of how ridiculous they are. The narrator is warm, witty, and occasionally exasperated. It can zoom from cosmic scale (the indifference of the universe) to intimate detail (the way Longjohn's hand finds the empty space where Blacky used to walk) within a single paragraph.

### The Narrator's Personality
- Warm but not sentimental. Finds the characters ridiculous and loves them for it.
- Can comment on things no character sees -- Lufnik's puddles and Kalmaris's mirror at the same time.
- Uses footnotes (blue boxes) for tangential observations, asides, and worldbuilding.
- Occasionally addresses the reader directly, but as a storyteller, not as a character. ("It should be noted that..." / "There is a particular quality to...")
- Knows the future but rarely spoils it -- may hint ("This would prove significant later" or "He would come to regret this") sparingly.
- The humor lives IN the narration, not just in dialogue.

### How the Narrator Handles Each Character
The narrator adjusts its prose style when the camera is close to a character:
- **Near Milo**: Prose becomes more scientific, more precise, more full of compound observations. Milo's analytical worldview colors the narration.
- **Near Kalmaris**: Prose becomes more theatrical, more rhythmic, more aware of how things look and sound.
- **Near Lamil**: Prose becomes sparser. Shorter sentences. Nature imagery.
- **Near Lufnik**: Prose becomes more controlled, more structured. Military cadence.
- **Near Longjohn**: Prose becomes rougher, more direct, more nautical.
- **Near Serox**: Prose becomes more declarative. Absolute statements. No hedging.

### Sample Narrator Voice

> The explosion was, by Milo Quickspark's careful estimation, approximately sixty-three percent not his fault. He would have been happy to explain the precise distribution of blame to anyone willing to listen, beginning with a detailed account of the reagent labels -- which he had written himself, three days prior, while simultaneously documenting the migratory patterns of cave spiders -- and the resulting handwriting, which had been, in his professional assessment, "adequate."
>
> Serox examined the smoking crater where the distillation apparatus had been. "The table was structurally unsound," he said. "Obviously."
>
> It was not obvious. The table had been fine. But Serox Kieselstein had never, in a hundred and twenty years of life, admitted to being wrong about anything, and he was not about to start now simply because the evidence was against him. Which it was. Comprehensively.

## Humor Blend Ratios

The novel blends three humor styles. The ratio shifts across the arc:

| Arc Position | Adams (Absurdist) | Pratchett (Warm Satire) | Sanderson (Earned Payoff) |
|-------------|-------------------|------------------------|--------------------------|
| Setup (Ch 1-5) | 40% | 50% | 10% |
| Rising (Ch 6-15) | 30% | 45% | 25% |
| Midpoint | 25% | 35% | 40% |
| Pre-Climax | 15% | 25% | 60% |
| Climax | 10% | 20% | 70% |
| Denouement | 30% | 50% | 20% |

## Prose Rules

### Paragraph Length
- **Maximum**: 80 words
- **Ideal**: 40-60 words
- **Action sequences**: 20-40 words
- **Dialogue exchanges**: Keep tight, 1-3 sentences per beat

### Chapter Length
- **Target**: 2,500 words
- **Minimum**: 2,000 words
- **Maximum**: 3,500 words
- **Climax chapters**: Up to 4,000 words

### Scene Structure
- Every scene needs: a goal, an obstacle, an outcome (yes-but / no-and)
- At least ONE genuine laugh per chapter
- At least ONE moment of genuine emotional connection per chapter
- End every chapter with a hook (question, revelation, or emotional punch followed by deflection)

---

## DO List
- Show magic through sensory detail (the smell of reagents, the heat of wildfire, the screaming of soul coins)
- Let humor emerge from character voice and situation, not from the narrator winking at the audience
- Use the blue box system for worldbuilding (Guide entries, alchemical notes, letters, journal entries)
- Give every character at least one moment per chapter where they feel like a real person
- Make combat emotional -- what's at stake matters more than what happens
- Plant details early that pay off later (Sanderson's "surprises that feel inevitable")
- Write dialogue that reveals character -- each person should be identifiable without dialogue tags
- Shift narrative prose style subtly depending on which character the camera is near
- Use the narrator's omniscience for dramatic irony (the reader sees what no single character can)

## DON'T List
- No D&D mechanics (no spell slots, no levels, no AC, no hit points, no dice references)
- No combat play-by-play (no "he swung his sword, she dodged, he swung again")
- No modern slang or anachronisms (unless deliberately for humor, clearly flagged)
- No narrator becoming a character -- the narrator observes and comments but does not participate
- No explaining jokes
- No "said bookisms" (he exclaimed, she retorted) -- use "said" or action beats
- No infodumps -- worldbuild through character reactions and blue boxes
- No perfect characters -- everyone fails, everyone has blind spots
- No head-hopping within a scene -- stay near one character per scene, shift at scene breaks

## Blue Box Format (Royal Road)

Blue boxes serve as Pratchett-style footnotes rendered for web fiction. With the omniscient narrator, they serve two purposes:

**DM's footnotes** -- The DM's own tangential observations, asides, and worldbuilding commentary. This is the primary Pratchett channel.

**Embedded documents** -- In-world texts quoted by the narrator:
1. **Milo's Journal** -- Excerpts from Milo's actual journal/logbook, quoted in his scientific voice. This preserves Milo's first-person analytical voice as a feature within the third-person narrative.
2. **The Somewhat Unreliable Guide to [Location]** -- Milo's travel guide entries (Volume 3)
3. **Letters & Documents** -- In-world correspondence
4. **Historical Asides** -- Brief lore drops

Format on Royal Road:
```
> **DM's Note:** [content]
```
```
> **From the journal of Milo Quickspark:** [content in Milo's first-person voice]
```

The DM is the omniscient narrator -- the person who built this world, knows its secrets, and is deeply fond of these characters while being fully aware of how ridiculous they are. The DM's Note label signals the story's D&D origins and distinguishes the narrator's voice from Milo's journal entries.
```

Blue boxes should appear 2-4 times per chapter. They should be funny, informative, or both. They should NEVER contain plot-critical information that the reader can't get elsewhere -- they're bonus content.

## Appendix: Voice Calibration Test Passage

This passage demonstrates the target omniscient narrator voice. Use it as a reference when drafting.

---

The explosion was, by Milo Quickspark's careful estimation, approximately sixty-three percent not his fault.

He would have been happy to explain the remaining thirty-seven percent to anyone willing to listen, beginning with a detailed account of the reagent labels -- which he had written himself, three days prior, while simultaneously documenting the migratory patterns of cave spiders -- and the environmental variable of Serox hitting the table with his hammer while Milo was measuring powdered sulphite. Milo considered this a significant contributing factor. Serox considered it irrelevant.

"The table was structurally unsound," Serox said, examining the smoking crater where the distillation apparatus had been. "Obviously."

It was not obvious. The table had been perfectly adequate. But Serox Kieselstein had never, in a hundred and twenty years of dwarven life, admitted to being wrong about anything, and the smoking ruins of a gnome's laboratory were not going to be the place he started.

> **DM's Note:** A careful observer -- and there were several in the room, though only one of them was taking notes -- might have noticed a pattern in the interactions between Milo Quickspark and Serox Kieselstein. Milo built things. Serox broke them. Milo explained that the things were not meant to be broken. Serox explained that the things had already been broken, and his intervention had merely revealed a pre-existing flaw. This conversational loop had occurred, by Milo's count, forty-seven times. He kept a tally in the margins of his journal. It was the closest thing he had to a hobby that didn't involve fire.

Kalmaris swept into the room with the theatrical urgency of a man who had been waiting for an excuse to make an entrance. His feathers -- still magnificent despite the smoke -- caught the remaining light in a way he had almost certainly practiced.

"My dear, small, combustible friend," he said, spreading his wings in a gesture that knocked two bottles off a shelf, "are you *dead*?"

"Not measurably," said Milo, which was the kind of answer that only an alchemist would consider reassuring.

"Excellent. Then perhaps you could explain why the building is on fire."

Milo looked up. The building was, from a purely thermodynamic standpoint, undergoing rapid uncontrolled oxidation in several locations. He was already composing his rebuttal when Lamil spoke from the corner, where he had been standing so still that everyone had forgotten he was there. Two words. The most he had said all day.

"It's fire."

Longjohn, who had been drinking through the entire incident with the focused determination of a man training for a championship, set down his flask. "The wind's changed," he observed, which was his way of saying that things were about to get either much better or much worse.

A puddle formed near Lufnik's boots. He stepped away from it quickly. Nobody noticed, which was exactly how Lufnik preferred it. "Acknowledged," he said to no one in particular, and straightened his posture, which was already straighter than most furniture.

Milo made a note in his journal: *Spontaneous moisture accumulation, source unknown, seventeenth occurrence. Recommend further investigation.*

Blobby wobbled on Milo's shoulder, turned a shade of orange that Milo had tentatively classified as "resigned amusement," and ate a piece of the exploded table.

There are days when science is its own reward. This was not, by any reasonable assessment, one of them.
