# Chapter 1: In Which We Begin at the Bottom

Milo Quickspark woke up face-down on a beach with sand in his teeth and a small, gelatinous creature sitting on the back of his head.

The creature was Blobby, his alchemical familiar, and the fact that Blobby was sitting on him rather than inside the reinforced carrying case Milo had specifically designed for maritime travel suggested that the voyage had not gone according to plan. The sand in his teeth supported this hypothesis. So did the significant quantity of ocean in his left boot.

He sat up. Blobby slid down his face with the resigned elasticity of a thing that has learned to accommodate sudden movements, and landed in his lap. He was a deep emerald green, which Milo had tentatively catalogued as "relieved but judgmental."

"I know," Milo said. "But I maintain that the ship was already listing before I attempted the distillation."

Blobby wobbled in a manner that Milo chose to interpret as agreement. It was not, in any observable sense, agreement. But Milo Quickspark had been interpreting Blobby's wobbles with absolute confidence and approximately sixty percent accuracy for the better part of three years, and he saw no reason to revisit his methodology now.

> **DM's Note:** A brief introduction may be in order. Milo Quickspark was a gnome, an alchemist, and -- by his own assessment -- a rather good cook. Two of these things were true. He had been born in Eldertree Glade, trained under the estimable Eldra Brightflask, and was a member in good standing of the Mechanists' Collective of Baldur's Gate. The "good standing" was contingent on his continued payment of guild dues and the collective agreement to never again mention the Incident with the Phosphorous.
>
> As for Blobby: he was an alchemical homunculus of uncertain taxonomy, created during what Milo would generously call an experiment and what his mentor had called "the single most irresponsible use of reagents she had witnessed in forty years of professional alchemy." He communicated through color changes, wobbling, and enthusiastic bouncing. Whether anyone had ever successfully decoded these signals remained an open question that Milo considered closed.

The beach was white sand and black rock, curving away in both directions toward dense tropical vegetation that seemed to begin precisely where comfort ended. The sky was blue in the aggressive, uncomplicated way that tropical skies often are, as though it had never heard of nuance. The remains of a ship -- their ship -- were scattered along the tideline like the aftermath of a particularly ambitious woodworking accident.

Milo was alive. Blobby was alive. His satchel, containing approximately two-thirds of his portable laboratory and a truly excellent recipe for fish stew, was still strapped across his chest.

The day was already improving.

* * *

The first person he found was the dwarf.

He was sitting on a piece of hull plating, arms crossed, examining the wreckage with the expression of a structural engineer surveying a condemned building. He had not, as far as could be determined, sustained any injury from the shipwreck beyond a general intensification of his baseline disapproval.

"The keel construction was fundamentally unsound," he said by way of greeting. He had the kind of voice that suggested he had never in his life begun a conversation with pleasantries, and saw no reason to start now. "Substandard timber. Inadequate joint reinforcement. I identified no fewer than three critical stress points during boarding."

"You identified structural deficiencies in the ship's hull during boarding?" Milo asked.

"Obviously."

"And you boarded anyway?"

He looked at Milo as though the gnome had asked whether water was wet. "I wanted to see which stress point would fail first."

> **DM's Note:** This was Serox Kieselstein, and he would become one of the most important people in Milo's life, though at that particular moment he was simply the most opinionated person on a beach. He hailed from the mountain kingdom of Zornstein, where the art of smithing was considered sacred and the art of admitting error was considered a betrayal of one's ancestors, one's craft, and the fundamental structural integrity of the universe.
>
> Serox did not see the world the way other people saw the world. Other people saw objects, situations, relationships. Serox saw load-bearing structures and failure points. A conversation was a bridge: it either held weight or it didn't. A person was either well-tempered steel or pig iron. He had not yet decided which one Milo was. He was leaning toward pig iron with unusual thermal properties.

In the months that followed, Milo would keep a careful tally of every instance in which Serox was demonstrably, unambiguously, spectacularly wrong about something and subsequently admitted it. The count would hold, with remarkable consistency, at zero.

* * *

The second person to arrive -- or rather, the second person to make an entrance, because there was a difference, and he was keenly aware of it -- announced himself with a sound that could only be described as a chord played on an instrument that had very recently been submerged in saltwater.

"Fear not!" declared the bird.

He was standing on a rock above them, wings spread wide to catch the light, which illuminated his feathers in a manner he had almost certainly been adjusting for several minutes before they noticed him. He was an Aarakocra -- a bird-person, for the uninitiated -- and he held a waterlogged guitar with the tenderness most people reserve for small children or extremely expensive glassware.

"I am Kalmaris," he announced, "bard of the Emerald Crow, voice of the Azure Main, and singer of--"

"Calamari?" said Serox.

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Dense. Pressurized. The kind of silence that, in alchemical circles, typically precedes a detonation.

"Kal," said the bird, in a voice that had dropped several octaves and gained several edges, "*mar*. *Is*."

"That's what I said."

"It was not."

"It was close enough."

It was not close enough. It would never be close enough. The mispronunciation of Kalmaris's name would become one of the great recurring irritations of the months ahead, suffered by the bard with a frequency that seemed, to any rational observer, statistically improbable. Milo would later note this in his journal. Kalmaris would later note it with his fists.

> **DM's Note:** A bard is not, despite popular misconception, merely a person who plays music. A bard is a person who believes -- with the absolute, unshakeable conviction of the deeply theatrical -- that the right performance can change reality. That a well-told story matters more than what actually happened. That if you sing loudly enough about being brave, the universe will eventually agree and make it true.
>
> Kalmaris believed this with every feather on his body. He had been the voice of the Emerald Crow, a pirate vessel of moderate infamy, and his songs had been the crew's heartbeat. Without them, he was a bird on a rock making noise. He did not yet know how much of that identity had already been taken from him, or what had taken it, or that the thing in the mirror was patient enough to wait.

> **From the journal of Milo Quickspark:** *Day 1. First interpersonal conflict. Elapsed time since group formation: approximately four minutes. Underlying cause: phonetic. Recommend further observation before drawing conclusions about long-term group dynamics.*

In retrospect, four minutes was actually quite good.

* * *

The elf emerged from the treeline as though he had grown there and simply decided to relocate.

He was tall and quiet in the way that old forests are quiet -- not empty, but full of things that had chosen not to speak. He looked at Milo. He looked at Serox. He looked at Kalmaris, who was still explaining, with considerable passion, the precise phonetic structure of his name.

"Lamil," said the elf.

That was his entire introduction. One word. It should be noted that this was, by Lamil's standards, a rather lengthy speech. He was a druid of the Circle of Wildfire, a leader of an ancient brotherhood, a man who communed with nature spirits and read the health of the world through the behavior of wind and water. He was also a man who saw no particular reason to share any of this information with strangers, and wouldn't for quite some time.

He walked to a patch of grass at the edge of the beach, sat down, and placed his hand flat against the earth. After a moment he closed his eyes. The grass beneath his hand, which had been trampled and salt-burned, straightened.

He opened his eyes. "The island is distressed," he said.

Nobody asked by what. In the experience of those present, people who say things like "the island is distressed" are rarely seeking follow-up questions, and the answers are rarely comforting.

> **DM's Note:** There is a common and entirely forgivable mistake that people make about quiet individuals. They assume the quiet person has nothing to say. In Lamil Morgenhauch's case, the opposite was true: he had a great deal to say, and had decided that none of it was any of your business.
>
> He had led the Ember Brotherhood for longer than most human civilizations had existed. He had spoken to trees that remembered the world before roads. He had watched forests burn and grow back different -- not weaker, not stronger, just changed -- and had learned from them that the most important responses are the ones that take a century to articulate. By those standards, conversation with people who expected replies within seconds was not difficult. It was simply pointless.
>
> The grass he healed would die again, of course. He knew that. He healed it anyway. This told you everything you needed to know about Lamil, if you were paying attention. Almost nobody was.

* * *

The fifth member of their future group arrived at a run.

He burst from the jungle with the controlled urgency of someone who had been trained to run toward danger but whose current trajectory was very clearly *away* from it. He stopped at the treeline, assessed the beach in a single sweep, and positioned himself with his back to a rock in a way that covered every approach simultaneously. It was the kind of thing a person does without thinking after years of military service, and Lufnik Springveil had done very little without thinking since the day the moon brought him back from the dead.

Not that anyone knew about that. Not that anyone was going to know about that, if he had anything to say about it.

"Hostiles?" asked Serox.

"Negative," Lufnik said. "Large insect life. Non-aggressive but..." He paused, searching for the military-appropriate term for what was, fundamentally, just "unpleasant." "...Tactically inadvisable to engage."

"You ran from bugs," said Kalmaris.

"I executed a strategic withdrawal from a position of disadvantage."

"You ran from *bugs*."

"They were very large bugs."

Behind him, at the waterline, something odd happened. The ocean, which had been observing a perfectly regular tidal pattern all morning, sent a single wave further up the beach than any wave before it -- a long, reaching tongue of saltwater that climbed the sand toward the rock where Lufnik stood, lapped against its base, and retreated. The next wave fell short by several feet, as though the ocean had remembered its boundaries and was embarrassed to have overstepped them.

Milo glanced at the wet mark on the sand. He attributed it to a rogue swell and thought nothing more of it.

This was a mistake, though not one he would recognize for quite some time.

> **DM's Note:** Every soldier carries two packs. The first is the one on their back -- the bedroll, the rations, the weapon oil, the spare bootlaces. This pack is heavy but knowable. Its weight can be measured and distributed and set down at the end of the day.
>
> The second pack is the one they carry inside. It contains the things that happened to them and the things they did and the distance between the person they were trained to be and the person they became instead. This pack cannot be set down. It does not get lighter with time. And it is invisible to everyone except other soldiers, who recognize it the way animals recognize their own kind -- not through any visible sign, but through the particular way a person holds themselves when they are used to bearing weight that no one else can see.
>
> Lufnik Springveil's second pack was heavier than most. He had died, once, and come back different. The difference was the kind of secret that leaks -- slowly, persistently, in ways he could not always control. He was very, very good at controlling it. But the ocean had noticed anyway. The ocean always does.

* * *

The last of them arrived with a boar.

They heard him before they saw him -- a low, rhythmic cursing that filtered through the jungle like a sea shanty stripped of its melody and left with only the profanity. The undergrowth parted to reveal a man who looked as though the sea had made him, used him hard, and then discarded him on this beach out of professional courtesy.

He was tall and weathered, with the kind of tan that suggested a lifetime on the water, and he carried himself with the rolling gait of someone whose legs had long ago accepted that the ground would never be as reliable as a ship's deck. Under one arm he held a flask. At his side trotted a boar -- black-bristled, compact, and eyeing them all with the wary intelligence of an animal that had learned not to trust first impressions.

"Longjohn," the man said, distributing facts rather than making introductions. He took a drink from his flask, assessed it critically with the evaluative intensity of a sommelier, and apparently found it acceptable. "Silver. The boar's Blacky. Don't touch him."

Blacky snorted. There was something proprietary about it, as though *he* was introducing *Longjohn*, and was reserving judgment about the rest of them.

> **DM's Note:** Longjohn Silver had two great talents. The first was an almost supernatural ability to identify any alcoholic beverage by smell, including its region of origin, approximate age, and -- in his words -- "moral character." The second was a capacity for silence so complete and so sudden that its appearance was louder than any sound he could make. The first talent was exercised frequently. The second was reserved for exactly one subject, and the day the others learned what that subject was would be the worst day of all their lives.
>
> But that was months away. On this first day, he was simply a man with a boar and a flask, and both suited him perfectly.

"And what do you *do*?" Kalmaris asked.

Longjohn looked at the bird-bard. Looked at the shipwreck. Looked at the vast and indifferent ocean that had deposited them all here like so much interesting debris.

"At the moment," he said, "I survive."

He sat down on a log, offered Blacky a scratch behind the ears that the boar accepted with grudging satisfaction, and produced a second, smaller flask from somewhere about his person. "Anyone want a drink? No? Suit yourselves."

He took a long, professional pull and settled in with the air of a man who had been shipwrecked before and was prepared to be deeply unimpressed by the experience.

* * *

Six strangers on a beach. A gnome, a dwarf, a bird, an elf, a soldier, and a pirate with a boar. They had no ship, no map, no plan, and no immediately obvious reason to trust each other with anything more consequential than the time of day.

Naturally, Milo decided to cook dinner.

"I have," he announced, pulling his kit from his satchel with the confidence of a man about to do something he genuinely believed he was good at, "a truly excellent recipe for tropical fish stew."

The reactions were varied. Serox grunted, which Milo interpreted as enthusiasm. Kalmaris raised a feathered eyebrow with the elegance of a theater critic. Lamil said nothing, which was consistent. Lufnik nodded once, precisely, and said "Acknowledged," as though Milo had reported a weather condition. Longjohn drank.

Blobby turned a vibrant, anticipatory orange.

The stew, Milo felt, was magnificent. He had adapted the recipe for available ingredients -- local shellfish, a tuber he identified as nutritionally promising, several aromatic leaves that Lamil confirmed were not poisonous, and a pinch of powdered firesalt from his personal supply. The color was admirable. The consistency was, to his professional eye, exactly right. The aroma was distinctive, which in alchemical circles is considered a compliment.

In culinary circles, it is not.

He served it with quiet pride. There was a silence. It was not, it must be said, the silence of anticipation.

"This is..." Kalmaris began. He was an expressive creature by nature, and the effort required to restrain that expressiveness was visible in every feather. "...an experience."

"Thank you," said Milo.

"I didn't say a good one."

"You didn't need to. Your face said it for you."

"My face is saying something very specific, yes."

Serox ate his entire bowl without comment. He had spent three years eating military rations that were, by his own assessment, "structurally adequate and gastronomically irrelevant." Milo's stew was at least interesting. Whether "interesting" was a positive quality in food remained, from his perspective, an open question that did not require further discussion.

Longjohn fed his portion to Blacky when he thought nobody was looking. Blacky ate it. Then Blacky ate the bowl.

Lufnik consumed his serving with the methodical efficiency of a soldier fueling for duty. If he had opinions about the taste, they were classified.

Lamil set his bowl down after one spoonful, very gently, the way one might set down something one wished to be kind to despite its many failings. He ate a handful of leaves from a nearby bush instead.

Blobby ate three helpings. But Blobby once ate a piece of volcanic glass, so his endorsement, while enthusiastic, was not strictly a measure of culinary quality.

"Tomorrow," Milo said, washing the pot with the satisfaction of a job well done, "I'm thinking a breakfast frittata. The island has excellent eggs, if the birds here are cooperative."

Another silence. Briefer this time. More practiced.

"Can't wait," said Kalmaris, in a tone that contained multitudes.

* * *

Night fell on Moreva -- quick and total, the way it does in the tropics, as though someone had simply turned the sky off. They arranged themselves around the remains of the cooking fire: six people who had been strangers that morning and were now something not yet definable. Not friends. Not allies. Just a group of people who had ended up in the same place through no particular plan, and who had not yet found a reason to leave.

The jungle beyond the firelight made sounds that defied comfortable classification. The ocean hissed against the sand. Somewhere in the trees, something moved that was larger than anyone was comfortable with.

> **From the journal of Milo Quickspark:** *Day 1. Shipwrecked on unknown island. Party of six. Preliminary assessment: diverse skill sets, variable temperaments, unclear group cohesion. The dwarf evaluates structural integrity. The bird-man evaluates acoustics. The elf evaluates ecosystems. The soldier evaluates threats. The pirate evaluates alcohol. I evaluate everything else. Recommend observation and patience.*
>
> *They seem nice. I hope they stay.*

Around the fire, the others were finding their places. Serox propped against a rock, arms folded, somehow managing to look disapproving even while unconscious. Kalmaris with his guitar cradled like a child. Lamil flat on the ground, face up, perfectly still, as though he were listening to the earth's pulse. Lufnik sitting upright with his back straight, because of course he was. Longjohn on his side with Blacky curled against his chest, one hand resting on the boar's bristled back.

The fire crackled. The stars came out -- so many of them, so bright, that even a gnome who preferred a well-lit laboratory had to admit they were doing excellent work.

From somewhere deep in the island's interior, something roared. It was faint, and far away, and it might have been the wind. But it didn't sound like wind. It sounded like something old, and very large, and profoundly unhappy.

Milo, who was not yet asleep, made a note in his journal: *Atmospheric disturbance. Possible geological activity. Investigate in morning.*

He closed the journal. He did not sleep for quite some time.

None of them did. But none of them said so, because they had not yet learned to trust each other with small vulnerabilities, let alone large ones. That would come later, and it would cost more than any of them expected.
