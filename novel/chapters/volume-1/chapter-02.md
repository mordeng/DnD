# Chapter 2: Regarding the Inadvisability of Tropical Cuisine

Morning arrived on Moreva the way mornings arrive in the tropics: suddenly, aggressively, and with an enthusiasm that nobody had requested.

Milo Quickspark woke to discover that three things had happened during the night. First, the tide had come in and rearranged several pieces of shipwreck into a pattern that, if viewed from above, would have resembled a rude gesture. Second, a species of crab he had never encountered before had colonized his left boot. Third, and most importantly, Blobby had eaten his spare socks.

The crabs were orange, the size of a fist, and apparently regarded gnomish footwear as prime real estate. Milo regarded them with the scientific fascination of a man whose foot was, at that moment, very cold and very wet.

"Fascinating," he said, carefully extracting a crab that had wedged itself into the toe. "Genus unknown, but the coloring suggests a diet heavy in carotenoids. Possibly related to the Sword Coast red-backed--"

"The frittata," said Kalmaris, from behind him.

"Hmm?"

"You promised a frittata."

There was a hunger in the bird-man's voice that had nothing to do with wanting food and everything to do with wanting the experience of the previous evening's stew to be overwritten by something -- anything -- less memorable. The others, Milo noticed, were awake and watching him with expressions that ranged from guarded (Lufnik) to resigned (Serox) to silently communing with grass (Lamil).

"Ah, yes," said Milo. "The frittata."

> **From the journal of Milo Quickspark:** *Day 2. Breakfast preparation. Available ingredients: local eggs (procured from nesting site identified by Lamil -- three species, varying quality), tubers (confirmed non-toxic), a variety of aromatic herbs (confirmation pending), and dried fish from yesterday's surplus. Blobby has consumed my firesalt reserve, necessitating the use of a substitute seasoning I am developing from crushed shell fragments and a mineral compound I discovered in the rocks near the tideline. Preliminary taste tests are promising.*
>
> *Note: Blobby turned an unusual shade of violet after consuming the firesalt. Monitoring.*

The frittata was, by Milo's professional assessment, a triumph of improvised cuisine.

This assessment was not shared by the others.

The problem, though nobody would say so to Milo's face, was not the technique. The technique was, if anything, impressive. He had constructed a makeshift pan from a piece of hull plating, identified three species of edible egg through careful comparison of shell porosity, and managed a consistent heat distribution using a fire he'd designed with the precision of a small furnace.

The problem was that Milo Quickspark had the palate of a man who had spent decades tasting alchemical compounds. His tongue had been exposed to substances that would make a lesser organ file a formal complaint. What registered to him as "a subtle hint of mineral complexity" registered to everyone else as "the unmistakable flavor of eating a beach."

Longjohn, who had been watching the preparation with the wary assessment of a man evaluating incoming weather, took one bite. He chewed. He swallowed. He looked at Blacky.

Blacky looked back with the steady, knowing gaze of an animal who had already calculated that he would be receiving a second breakfast.

"The wind's changed," Longjohn said, which meant absolutely nothing in context but allowed him to stand up and walk away from the frittata with an air of nautical purpose.

Blacky ate the rest of Longjohn's portion. Then he ate Lufnik's portion, which Lufnik had set down with military precision at the exact moment Milo looked away. Then he ate the serving bowl, which was made of wood and therefore, by Blacky's culinary standards, more appetizing than the frittata had been.

It should be understood that Blacky was not a picky eater. He was a boar of catholic tastes and robust constitution, the kind of creature who regarded the distinction between "food" and "not food" as a matter of personal philosophy rather than biological necessity. He had, during his years with Longjohn on the island where they had found each other, eaten bark, seashells, rope, and on one memorable occasion, a hat. His willingness to consume Milo's cooking was therefore not the endorsement that Milo believed it to be.

But Longjohn never said this. He never would.

* * *

They went into the jungle because the alternative was to stay on the beach, and staying on the beach meant more cooking.

Nobody said this either.

"We should establish our geographic position," Milo said, pulling out his journal and a stick of charcoal. "Determine available resources. Map potential hazards. Identify--"

"Find water," said Lamil.

It was, as usual, the only contribution the elf made to the planning discussion, and it was, as usual, the only contribution that mattered. They had seawater in abundance and fresh water in none, and no amount of alchemical ingenuity could change the fundamental chemical reality of salt.

They entered the jungle in a loose formation that said everything about who they were and nothing about any agreed-upon strategy. Lufnik took the lead, because soldiers lead. Serox walked beside him, because dwarves do not follow. Kalmaris drifted along the flank, occasionally pausing to examine a flower or a butterfly with the theatrical appreciation of a man who wanted everyone to notice his sensitivity. Milo walked in the middle, taking notes. Lamil walked wherever the trees were thickest, which meant he frequently disappeared entirely.

Longjohn walked at the back with Blacky.

There is something particular about the way a man walks with an animal he loves. It is not the stride of an owner or the caution of a handler. It is the easy, synchronized rhythm of two creatures who have learned each other's pace so thoroughly that they no longer think about it. Longjohn's hand occasionally dropped to scratch behind Blacky's ear, and the boar would lean into the touch without breaking step, and neither of them appeared to notice they were doing it.

They had found each other on an island not unlike this one. They had survived together when surviving alone would have been easier, because easier was not the same thing as bearable. The details of that story were Longjohn's to keep, and he kept them behind the particular silence he reserved for things that mattered too much to be spoken aloud.

Milo, watching from behind his notebook, wrote: *The pirate and his boar appear to share a bond of significant depth. Further observation required to determine the nature and extent of this relationship.*

It was, in its way, the most perceptive thing Milo had written in years. He just didn't know it yet.

* * *

The jungle, for its part, was watching them.

Not in any way that could be empirically verified, of course. The trees were trees. The vines were vines. The insects were insects, albeit insects of a size and disposition that made Lufnik's "strategic withdrawal" of the previous day seem increasingly reasonable.

But Lamil felt it.

He had stopped beside a tree whose trunk was wider than Serox was tall. His hand rested against the bark, and his eyes were closed, and for a long moment the only sound was the drip of humidity and the distant argument between Kalmaris and Serox about the best route to high ground.

"Something is wrong here," he said.

Milo, who was nearest, looked up from his notes. "Wrong how?"

Lamil was quiet for long enough that Milo began to wonder if the question had been rhetorical. Then: "The forest is afraid."

"Afraid of what?"

Another long pause. Lamil opened his eyes. "If it knew," he said, "it would not be afraid."

> **DM's Note:** There is a common misconception that druids talk to plants in the way that normal people talk to each other -- with words, and sentences, and the reasonable expectation of a reply. This is not how it works. A druid's communion with nature is less a conversation and more an exchange of impressions: a shift in air pressure here, a change in root growth there, the slow, arboreal equivalent of a shrug or a flinch.
>
> Lamil Morgenhauch was exceptionally good at this. He could read the health of a forest the way Milo could read a chemical reaction -- through subtle indicators that most people would dismiss as background noise. And what the forest on Moreva was telling him, in the slow, patient vocabulary of ancient trees, was something that translated roughly as: *Please leave. Quickly. We would like you to not be here.*
>
> Forests, as a rule, do not ask people to leave. They are patient, indifferent, and profoundly unconcerned with the comings and goings of creatures who measure their lives in decades rather than centuries. When a forest is afraid, something has gone very wrong indeed.

They found fresh water in a stream that cascaded down a rocky hillside into a pool of startling clarity. Milo tested it for common contaminants, found none, and declared it potable. They drank. They refilled their waterskins. Blobby fell into the pool and turned a shade of blue that suggested surprise.

It was at the pool that Longjohn proved his other value.

"Someone's been here," he said, studying the mud at the water's edge with the focused attention of a man reading a map. "Footprints. Multiple. Coming from the north. Regular traffic -- this isn't exploration, this is a path."

"You can determine all that from mud?" Milo asked.

Longjohn gave him a look that suggested the question was beneath them both. "Spent two years on an island reading tracks," he said. "Boar tracks, bird tracks, weather patterns, tide marks. Mud tells you everything if you know how to listen."

Blacky, who had been snuffling at the same mud, looked up and snorted.

"Blacky agrees," Longjohn said.

"You can understand the boar?" Kalmaris asked.

"Can you not?"

This was apparently not a joke. Longjohn followed the tracks north. Blacky followed Longjohn. After a moment's hesitation -- during which Serox declared that the rock formation by the pool was "structurally interesting" and Lamil touched another tree and looked unhappy -- the rest of them followed too.

The tracks led through a gap in the undergrowth that had clearly been widened by regular foot traffic. The vegetation on either side was cut, not broken -- someone had been maintaining this path. Recently.

They walked for perhaps an hour. The jungle thinned. The air changed -- less the heavy, wet breath of the deep forest and more the smoky complexity of wood fires and human habitation.

Then the trees opened, and they saw the village.

It was not abandoned. Smoke rose from cooking fires. People moved between structures. From a distance, it looked like any small coastal settlement going about its daily business.

Milo felt a surge of relief so profound it was almost alchemical. People meant information, resources, and the possibility of rescue. He quickened his pace.

Behind him, Lamil stopped walking.

"Wait," the elf said.

It was only one word. But it was the loudest word Milo had ever heard Lamil speak, and there was something in it -- a tightness, a resistance -- that made everyone stop where they stood.

"What is it?" Lufnik asked. His hand, Milo noticed, had moved to where a weapon would be.

Lamil's eyes were on the village. His face was perfectly still, but there was a tension in his jaw that hadn't been there before. When he spoke, his voice was very quiet.

"The trees won't go near it."

Milo looked. It was true. The jungle, which grew with aggressive enthusiasm everywhere else on the island, stopped in an almost perfect line twenty meters from the village's edge. Not the gradual thinning of a cleared area -- the abrupt halt of vegetation that had decided, collectively, to grow no further.

As if the forest itself was keeping its distance.

"Well," said Kalmaris, with the determinedly cheerful tone of a man who has noticed that everything is fine and would like to continue believing this for as long as possible, "that's probably nothing."

It was not nothing. But they were six strangers with no ship, no supplies, and a gnome who was already planning what to cook for the village, so they walked toward it anyway.

Some decisions look brave in retrospect. Others just look inevitable.
