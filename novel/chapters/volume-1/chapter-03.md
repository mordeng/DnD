# Chapter 3: A Scientific Approach to Mass Hysteria

The village of Moreva -- which, they would learn, shared its name with the island, because the inhabitants had either never needed to distinguish between the two or had long since stopped caring about the distinction -- contained approximately forty people, twelve structures, and one problem that nobody wanted to discuss.

The twelve structures were unremarkable: wooden frames, thatched roofs, the kind of practical construction that suggested a community more interested in living than in architecture. Serox evaluated them as "adequate," which was, by dwarven standards, lavish praise. The forty people were a mix of fishermen, farmers, and artisans -- exactly the kind of small, self-sufficient settlement you might expect to find on a remote tropical island.

The problem was that roughly a third of them had lost their minds.

This was not immediately apparent. The village chief, a weathered woman named Hera who carried herself with the quiet authority of someone accustomed to solving problems through competence rather than volume, welcomed them with the careful hospitality of a person who was glad for help but too proud to ask for it directly.

"Travelers are rare," she said, leading them toward the village's central clearing. "The currents keep most ships away. Those that make it..." She glanced at the ocean, and something flickered across her face. "Most don't stay long."

"We didn't exactly choose to stay," Longjohn said. "Our ship chose for us."

"Yes. They usually do."

> **DM's Note:** It is worth noting that Chief Hera did not seem surprised by the shipwreck. She did not seem surprised by anything. She had the demeanor of a woman who had, at some point in the recent past, exceeded her lifetime capacity for surprise and was now operating in a state of permanent unsurprisability -- which, as Milo would eventually discover, was an entirely rational response to the circumstances.

They were given food -- good food, Milo noted with the mild bewilderment of a man who did not understand why other people's cooking always seemed to receive more enthusiastic responses than his own. They were shown to an empty structure where they could sleep. They were treated with a kindness that was genuine and a wariness that was equally genuine, and it was the wariness that Milo found more interesting.

"Something is wrong here," he said to the group that evening, after Hera had left. He was sitting cross-legged on the floor with his journal open, his charcoal moving in the quick, precise strokes of a mind organizing observations into categories.

"Yes," said Lamil.

"The forest--"

"Yes."

"And the people--"

"Yes."

Lamil was not, it became clear, going to elaborate. Milo turned to the others.

"I've been observing the villagers. Their behavior is... inconsistent." He consulted his notes. "The fisherman we passed on the way in was mending a net. Normal behavior. Except that the net had no holes. He was mending a perfectly intact net, and he appeared to have been doing so for several hours."

"Maybe he's thorough," Kalmaris offered.

"The woman at the well drew water, carried it to her door, poured it on the ground, and went back to draw more. She did this four times while I was watching."

"Maybe she's watering the--"

"There was nothing growing there. It was bare dirt."

Silence. Then Longjohn: "Saw something too. Man sitting outside his house, talking to a chair. Not a person in a chair. The chair. Having a full conversation with it, complete with pauses for the chair's responses."

"The chair," said Serox, "was not responding."

"Obviously the chair was not responding. It was a chair."

"Then why did you feel the need to clarify?"

"Because on this island, I'm not taking anything for granted."

> **From the journal of Milo Quickspark:** *Day 2, evening. Initial observations of Moreva village populace. Approximately 30-35% of observed inhabitants display behaviors inconsistent with baseline cognitive function. Symptoms include: repetitive purposeless action, conversation with inanimate objects, apparent memory loss (one subject introduced himself to the same person three times in twenty minutes), emotional volatility without visible cause, and what I am tentatively classifying as "pattern blindness" -- the inability to recognize that one's own behavior is abnormal.*
>
> *Working hypothesis: environmental toxin affecting neural function. Possible sources: water supply (to be tested), local flora (to be catalogued), atmospheric compound (to be measured). Note: Lamil reports the forest itself is "afraid." Cross-reference with environmental analysis.*
>
> *Alternative hypothesis: disease, curse, or external influence of unknown nature. Insufficient data. Require further investigation.*
>
> *Priority: determine if condition is contagious.*

That last note, underlined twice, was the one that changed the mood in the room.

"Contagious?" Kalmaris said, and for a moment his theatrical composure slipped into something more honest. "You think we could catch this?"

"I think," Milo said carefully, "that we arrived on an island where a significant portion of the population is experiencing cognitive deterioration, and we don't yet know why. Until we do, caution seems prudent."

"Prudent," repeated Lufnik, as though the word were a military order. He straightened slightly. "Agreed. Recommend establishing baseline behavioral observations for all group members. Any deviation from established patterns should be reported immediately."

"You want us to tattle on each other if someone starts acting strange?" Longjohn summarized.

"Essentially."

"In a group where a bird performs to rocks, a dwarf argues with walls, an elf talks to trees, and a gnome thinks he can cook? How exactly would we notice a deviation?"

He had a point. Nobody acknowledged it, but he had a point.

* * *

Milo began his investigation the next morning with the systematic enthusiasm of a man who had been given a puzzle and a notebook and was therefore, by gnomish standards, in paradise.

He tested the water supply first. Three wells, one stream. He took samples from each, subjected them to the portable alchemical analysis kit he carried everywhere, and found nothing unusual. Clean water. Normal mineral content. No traces of known toxins, psychoactive compounds, or alchemical contaminants.

He tested the soil. He tested the cooking fires for unusual fuel sources. He catalogued every plant within the village boundary and compared them against his mental index of species known to cause cognitive effects. Nothing. Nothing. Nothing.

"The frustrating thing," he told Blobby, who was sitting on his shoulder and had turned a contemplative shade of teal, "is that the absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Something is causing this. My methods are simply not detecting it yet."

Blobby wobbled.

"You're right. I should expand the sample set."

It is unclear whether Blobby had, in fact, suggested expanding the sample set. But Milo found it useful to have these conversations, and Blobby found it useful to be included, and the arrangement suited them both.

He spent the afternoon interviewing villagers. Those who were lucid -- approximately two-thirds of the population -- described the condition's onset with a uniformity that Milo found both helpful and unsettling.

"It starts with forgetting," said an old fisherman who was, reassuringly, mending a net that actually had holes. "Small things first. Where you put your cup. What you were doing before lunch. Then bigger things. Your children's names. How to cook. What year it is."

"And then?"

The fisherman looked at the man sitting across the clearing, the one who was having a conversation with a chair. "And then you stop knowing you've forgotten."

"When did this begin?"

The fisherman thought about it. Thought about it some more. A crease appeared between his eyebrows. "I don't remember," he said, and then looked at Milo with a sudden, sharp awareness. "That's not a good sign, is it?"

"It's a data point," Milo said, because he had learned long ago that the truth could be delivered in clinical packaging without being any less true. "Data points are always useful."

He wrote in his journal that night: *The forgetting is progressive and, apparently, indiscriminate in its targets. Age, health, occupation -- no apparent correlation. The only pattern I've found is geographic: those closest to the village center seem more severely affected than those on the periphery.*

He drew a rough map. The village center. Rings of diminishing severity radiating outward. A bullseye, with the worst cases at the center.

Something was at the center, or below it, or connected to it.

Something was feeding.

* * *

The first shadow-wrongness happened on the afternoon of the third day.

It was subtle enough that Milo almost missed it, which was notable because Milo almost never missed anything. He was sitting outside their borrowed structure, updating his notes, when a woman walked past carrying a basket of fruit. The sun was behind her, casting her shadow long and dark across the dirt.

She stopped to adjust the basket. Her shadow did not.

For perhaps half a second, the shadow continued moving -- a fluid, sliding motion, like ink running downhill -- while the woman stood still. Then it snapped back into position, aligned perfectly with her posture, as though it had never been anywhere else.

Milo stared at the spot where the shadow had been. His hand was frozen over his journal, his charcoal hovering above the page. The analytical part of his mind was already generating explanations: optical illusion caused by heat distortion, parallax effect from the angle of observation, simple fatigue-induced misperception.

The rest of his mind -- the part that wasn't analytical, the small, ancient, gnomish part that knew when something was wrong the way an animal knows when a storm is coming -- that part said nothing. It just got very quiet.

He looked down at his journal and wrote, in very small handwriting: *Shadow anomaly. Single occurrence. Likely explainable. Monitoring.*

He did not write what he was thinking, which was that the shadow had moved in a direction that had nothing to do with the sun, and that it had moved *toward the center of the village*, and that it had moved the way things move when they are going home.

That night, around the fire, he told the others what he had seen.

"A shadow that moved on its own," Serox repeated flatly. "You're sure you weren't looking at it wrong?"

"I'm an alchemist. Precise observation is literally my vocation."

"Maybe your vocation needs new spectacles."

"I don't wear spectacles."

"Then your eyes need spectacles. The point is--"

"The point," said Lamil, and everyone stopped, because Lamil interrupting was so unusual that it carried the conversational weight of someone firing a cannon, "is that the island is sick. The people are forgetting. The trees are afraid. And now the shadows are wrong."

Three sentences. It was, by a significant margin, the most Lamil had said since they'd arrived.

Into the silence that followed, Milo said: "I'm going to need more notebooks."

Kalmaris laughed. Then Longjohn laughed. Then, remarkably, Serox made a sound that might have been a laugh if it hadn't been disguised as a cough. Lamil's mouth twitched, which was, by his standards, a standing ovation.

It was not, Milo reflected later, the moment they became friends. But it was the moment that the possibility of friendship stopped being theoretical and became, in the alchemical sense, a viable reaction.

> **From the journal of Milo Quickspark:** *Day 3, late. Working hypothesis updated. Environmental toxin theory: insufficient supporting evidence. New primary hypothesis: external influence of unknown nature, possibly centered on or beneath the village, affecting cognitive function and -- if today's observation is accurate -- the behavior of light and shadow.*
>
> *This hypothesis is, I note, significantly more alarming than the toxin theory. The toxin theory suggested a problem with a knowable, addressable cause. The shadow theory suggests something else entirely.*
>
> *I am not, as a rule, given to alarm. But I have made sure to check that my shadow is behaving itself. Twice.*

**Author's Note:**

Writing a mystery through the lens of a character who insists on treating everything as a research paper is one of my favorite challenges. Milo's approach to horror is to measure it, catalogue it, and assign it a working hypothesis. The horror doesn't care about his methodology. It's going to happen anyway.

The shadows are only going to get worse. Chapter 4 brings mirrors into the equation, which is bad news for everyone but especially for a certain bird-bard who's already having a complicated day. See you Wednesday.
