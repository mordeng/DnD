# Chapter 4: The Properties of Unnatural Darkness

On the morning of the fourth day, the animals left.

It happened all at once, which was the unsettling part. Birds in mid-song cut themselves short and flew, not in any particular direction but simply *away*. A dog that had been sleeping in a patch of sun stood up, shook itself, and trotted toward the treeline without looking back. The island's population of orange crabs -- which Milo had been cataloguing with increasing fondness -- retreated from the village boundary in a coordinated lateral migration that suggested either collective intelligence or collective terror.

Milo watched the exodus from the doorway of their borrowed structure, charcoal hovering over his journal, and felt the specific frustration of a man whose research subjects were leaving before he'd finished studying them.

"The crabs are retreating," he said to Blobby, who was sitting on his shoulder and had turned a shade of grey-green that Milo tentatively classified as "concerned."

Blobby wobbled.

"You're right. Coordinated flight responses in multiple species typically indicate a shared environmental stressor. Earthquake, perhaps. Or a change in atmospheric pressure." He paused. "Or something that animals can sense and alchemists cannot, which is, from a purely professional standpoint, humbling."

Blobby wobbled again, in a manner that suggested humility was not the emotion he had been attempting to communicate.

> **DM's Note:** Animals, as a general rule, are considerably better at knowing when to leave than people are. This is because animals evaluate danger with the elegant simplicity of a system that has only two outputs: *safe* and *not safe*. Humans -- and gnomes, and dwarves, and the occasional bird-person -- have complicated this system with nuance, curiosity, and the persistent belief that understanding a threat somehow makes it less threatening. The animals of Moreva did not understand the shadow beneath the village. They simply understood that they wanted to be somewhere else, and they acted on this information with an efficiency that the six strangers watching them could have learned from.

* * *

Kalmaris noticed the mirror first.

Or, more precisely, the mirror noticed Kalmaris first. But nobody knew that yet, least of all the bard himself, who was at that moment engaged in the daily ritual of preening his feathers using a piece of polished metal he had salvaged from the shipwreck. It was not a mirror in any formal sense -- just a fragment of hull plating that happened to be reflective enough to serve. Kalmaris had adopted it with the possessive tenderness of a man reunited with a long-lost friend.

He was adjusting the angle of his crest -- a precise, practiced motion that he would insist was about aerodynamics but was transparently about vanity -- when the reflection shifted.

It was the kind of shift that happens at the edge of perception. A flicker, like a candle flame in a draft. In the polished metal, Kalmaris's reflection turned its head a fraction of a second before Kalmaris did.

He stopped. Held very still. Looked at the reflection.

The reflection looked back. It was him. It was exactly him. The same feathers, the same tilt of the head, the same careful arrangement of plumage. There was absolutely nothing wrong with it.

Except that for a moment -- just a moment, the duration of a held breath -- the reflection's eyes had been darker than his. Not a different color. A different *depth*. As though something behind the reflection's eyes had been looking out, assessing, and deciding.

The metal cracked.

It was not a loud crack. Not the sharp report of glass shattering or the grinding complaint of stressed metal. It was a small, precise split that ran from the upper corner of the fragment to its center, bisecting the reflection's face in a clean diagonal line.

Kalmaris dropped the fragment as though it had bitten him.

"Defective material," Serox said from across the room, where he had been observing with the clinical disinterest of a dwarf who considered all non-dwarven metallurgy fundamentally inadequate. "Stress fracture. Thermal cycling from the shipwreck weakened the crystalline structure. Obviously."

"Obviously," Kalmaris repeated, but his voice had lost its theatrical warmth. It was flat. Simple. The voice of a man who had seen something he was not yet ready to name.

He picked the fragment up again. The crack ran through his reflected face like a scar. The reflection was, now, behaving itself perfectly. It moved when he moved. It stopped when he stopped. The eyes were the correct depth.

He put it in his pack and said nothing more about it.

> **From the journal of Milo Quickspark:** *Day 4. Curious incident: Kalmaris's improvised mirror developed a spontaneous stress fracture during routine use. While Serox's thermal cycling hypothesis is plausible, I note that the fracture pattern is inconsistent with typical thermal stress -- it is too clean, too straight, and follows the axis of the reflection rather than the grain of the metal. I would expect thermal fractures to propagate along the path of least molecular resistance, not along aesthetically significant lines.*
>

* * *

Milo spent the morning documenting every reflective surface in the village.

There were not many. Moreva was not the kind of settlement that invested in vanity -- the people had the practical disinterest in appearance that comes from living on an island where the only audience was each other and the crabs. But there were water troughs, and polished stone, and the occasional piece of metal, and Milo examined them all with the systematic enthusiasm of a man who had found a new avenue of inquiry.

Three of the water troughs had cracks in them. The cracks were recent -- the wood was pale and unstained at the fracture points. One of the polished stones outside the village chief's dwelling had split cleanly in two, and when Milo asked Hera about it, she looked at the stone and said, "It was whole yesterday," with the calm finality of a woman who had stopped being surprised by things that should have been surprising.

"Has this happened before?" Milo asked. "The cracking?"

Hera looked at him for a long time. There was something in her expression -- not suspicion, not fear, but the weary calculation of someone deciding how much truth a stranger could carry.

"Things break here," she said. "They have been breaking for some time."

"Things break everywhere. That's entropy."

"Not like this." She paused. "Not from the inside."

That phrase -- *from the inside* -- went into Milo's journal underlined three times and circled twice. Things did not break from the inside. Materials fractured along stress points, responding to external forces. Internal fracturing without external force implied a source of energy within the material itself, which was, from a thermodynamic standpoint, deeply unsettling.

Unless, of course, something was putting the energy there.

* * *

The water was the thing Lufnik noticed.

He did not mention it. Lufnik was not, as a general principle, a man who mentioned things. He observed, he catalogued, he filed his observations in the orderly internal archive of a soldier trained to assess and report, and then he did not report. Because reporting would mean explaining, and explaining would mean questions, and questions would lead, eventually, inevitably, to the one answer he could not give.

But the water in Moreva was wrong.

He had noticed it first at the well. He'd drawn a bucket -- standard procedure, hydration being a tactical necessity -- and the water had risen to meet him. Not dramatically. Not in a way anyone else would have seen. But the surface of the water in the well had climbed an inch before his hand reached the rope, and it had settled back down with a reluctance that water should not possess.

He had attributed it to wind. Wind affected water levels. This was basic meteorology.

Except that there was no wind.

Then, that afternoon, he walked past one of the cracked water troughs and the water inside it trembled. Not the gentle vibration of a footstep -- a directed trembling, the water's surface tilting toward him like a compass needle finding north.

He walked past it again, taking the longer route. The water tracked him.

Lufnik stopped. Looked at the trough. The water looked back with the patient attentiveness of a substance that was waiting for him to acknowledge something he had been ignoring for years.

He took a step backward. The water settled.

A small puddle formed near his left boot. Just a little one. Barely noticeable. He stepped over it and walked away, his spine very straight and his hands very still at his sides.

> **DM's Note:** There is a particular cruelty in being afraid of yourself. External threats can be fought, or fled from, or at the very least complained about in the company of friends. But a man who is frightened of the thing he carries inside him has no such recourse. Lufnik Springveil had been gifted a power he had not asked for, by a moon he had not prayed to (except once, dying, when prayer was not faith but reflex), and every ripple in every puddle was a reminder that he was not entirely the person he presented to the world.
>
> He was, in this way, a perfect match for an island where things were breaking from the inside.

* * *

The shadows were worse now. Milo had documented seven separate incidents by nightfall -- shadows that lagged behind their owners, shadows that stretched in directions unrelated to the light source, one shadow that had detached entirely from a sleeping villager and slid three feet across the ground before reattaching.

The incidents were increasing in frequency and severity. They were also, Milo noted with the careful alarm of a man trying not to alarm himself, clustered around the village center. His bullseye map was no longer a hypothesis. It was a heat map.

"Something is in the center of this village," he told the group that night. "Or beneath it. The shadow behavior, the mirror fractures, the animal exodus -- they all point toward a single source. The affected radius is approximately one hundred meters and shrinking. The intensity is increasing. Whatever is causing this is getting stronger, and it's getting stronger *quickly*."

"How quickly?" Lufnik asked. Milo appreciated the question. It was the kind of question a soldier asked -- precise, functional, requiring a quantifiable answer.

"Based on the rate of symptom progression in the villagers and the increase in shadow anomalies over the past forty-eight hours, I estimate..." He consulted his notes. Consulted them again. "Days. Perhaps a week. Perhaps less."

"Perhaps less," Longjohn repeated. He was sitting apart from the others, running a whetstone along a blade with the slow, rhythmic strokes of a man thinking about something other than his knife. Blacky lay beside him, his snout resting on Longjohn's boot, eyes half-closed but ears alert. "And when it finishes getting stronger?"

Nobody answered. The fire crackled. The jungle beyond the village was quieter than it should have been -- the animals had taken their noise with them when they left.

"The villagers will forget everything," Milo said, because someone had to. "And then... I don't know. I don't have enough data."

"You have a hypothesis," Lamil said.

Everyone looked at the elf. It was two more words than anyone had expected from him at this hour.

Milo looked at his journal. At the bullseye map, the shadow vectors, the fracture patterns, the careful measurements of progressive cognitive decline. He looked at what the data was telling him and at what was sitting in the spaces between the data, the shape defined by everything he couldn't measure.

"Something is feeding on them," he said. "Something in the dark, beneath the village, is eating their memories, and it's using the shadows to do it. And every day it eats more, it gets stronger, and the shadows reach further."

The silence that followed was not the comfortable silence of a group that has nothing to say. It was the pressurized silence of a group that has too much.

"Well," said Kalmaris, pulling his wings tight around himself in a gesture that was partly theatrical and partly genuinely cold, "at least we know what we're dealing with."

"We don't," said Milo. "We know what it does. We don't know what it is."

"From a purely strategic standpoint," said Lufnik, and then stopped, because even he could not immediately find a military framework for shadows that eat memories.

Serox grunted. "We go to the center. We find it. We break it."

"That's not a plan," said Kalmaris.

"It's a dwarven plan."

"That explains a great deal about dwarven architecture."

"Dwarven architecture has stood for three thousand years."

"So has mold."

Longjohn laughed. It was a short, rough sound, expelled more than offered, but it was genuine, and it broke the tension the way a stone breaks the surface of still water.

"We go to the center," he said. "We find it." He resheathed the knife. "And then we figure out the rest."

It was, Milo reflected, not so much a plan as the absence of any better option. But it was something they could do together, and doing something together was, at that moment, more important than doing it well.

> **From the journal of Milo Quickspark:** *Day 4, late. Updated hypothesis: parasitic entity of unknown nature, located at or beneath village center, feeding on cognitive function through shadow-based extraction. Entity is growing stronger. Timeline for full village incapacitation: 5-7 days, possibly less.*
>
> *New anomaly: reflective surfaces cracking without external stress. Possibly connected to shadow phenomenon. Possibly connected to nothing. The distinction matters.*
>
> *Personal note: checked my own reflection four times today. It behaved normally each time. I am choosing to find this reassuring.*
>
> *I am not entirely reassured.*

**Author's Note:**

Kalmaris's mirror scene is one of those moments I've had planned since the story bible. The reflection that moves a fraction too early -- that's the first thread of something that won't fully unravel until Volume 3. Serox dismissing it as a stress fracture is the most Serox response possible, and also exactly the kind of rational explanation that makes the irrational feel worse by contrast.

Lufnik's water scene was harder to write. His relationship with his own power is so deeply internalized that showing it without explaining it requires a delicate touch. The water reaching for him -- not responding to him, but *reaching* -- felt right. He's not controlling it. It's recognizing him.

Chapter 5 puts these people together for the first time as a team. This goes about as well as you'd expect. See you Friday.
