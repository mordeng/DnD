# Chapter 5: On the Subject of Teamwork (A Preliminary Study)

The argument began, as the best arguments do, with a fundamental disagreement about what constituted a door.

They had gathered at the village center that morning -- all six of them, plus Blobby and Blacky, because nobody had thought to establish a policy on whether familiars and combat boars counted as team members. (They did. Nobody questioned this. Questioning a boar's right to be present was, by unspoken consensus, not worth the boar's response.)

The village center contained a well, a fire pit, a communal cooking area, and a stone platform that Milo had identified as the epicenter of the shadow activity. The platform was old -- older than the village, older than the trees that refused to approach it, old in the way that things are old when they have been waiting for something. Its surface was covered in carvings that might have been decorative or might have been functional, and the distinction mattered enormously.

"There's a chamber beneath this," Serox said. He had been on his knees for twenty minutes, running his fingers along the stone's edges with the focused intensity of a man reading braille in a language he'd invented. "The acoustic resonance is consistent with a hollow space approximately four meters below grade. The stonework is excellent. Pre-Foundational period, I'd estimate. Dwarven-influenced, if not dwarven-made."

"Can you open it?" Milo asked.

"Obviously."

"How?"

Serox paused. It was the particular pause of a man who had committed to confidence before confirming it was warranted. "There will be a mechanism. There is always a mechanism. Dwarven-influenced construction does not rely on brute force -- it employs integrated engineering solutions that respond to specific inputs."

"So you don't know how," said Longjohn.

"I said there will be a mechanism."

"Which you haven't found."

"Which I have not *yet* found. The distinction is important."

"The distinction is that we're standing on a rock instead of under it."

> **DM's Note:** It is a truth universally acknowledged by anyone who has ever attempted to coordinate six independently-minded people toward a single objective that the hardest part is not the objective. The hardest part is the six people. Each of them arrives with their own assessment of the situation, their own preferred methodology, and their own absolute certainty that everyone else's approach is, at best, inefficient and, at worst, evidence of a fundamental character flaw. The fact that they are usually all partially right makes the situation considerably worse.

Serox's approach was structural. Find the mechanism, understand the engineering, open the door in the manner its builders intended. This was, from a dwarven perspective, the only civilized option. The alternative -- breaking through -- was not considered an alternative so much as an act of cultural vandalism.

Longjohn's approach was practical. The rock was between them and the thing they needed to reach. The rock should stop being between them and the thing they needed to reach. How this was achieved was a matter of results, not philosophy.

"We could dig around it," Longjohn said.

"You don't *dig around* Dwarven-influenced stonework."

"Why not?"

"Because it's *Dwarven-influenced stonework*."

"That's not a reason, that's a description."

Serox stood up. He was not a tall man -- being a dwarf, he would have been deeply offended by the suggestion that height was a relevant metric for anything -- but he could project physical presence with the efficiency of a much larger creature. "The mechanism exists," he said, in the voice he used for things that were not open to discussion, which was all of his voices. "I will find it. Digging is not an option."

"Lad," said Longjohn, and Serox's jaw tightened at the word, because he was a hundred and twenty years old and had been crafting steel since before Longjohn's grandparents were born, "there are people up here losing their minds. Every day we stand around appreciating the architecture, more of them go."

"And every day you rush into something you don't understand, you risk making it worse. As I have already explained."

"You haven't explained anything. You've told us the rock is impressive. We're all very impressed. Now open the bloody rock."

They were, Milo noted with scientific detachment, approximately thirty seconds from a physical altercation. He could see the calculations running behind both sets of eyes -- Longjohn's quick assessment of angles and reach, Serox's slower but more deliberate evaluation of structural weak points (on Longjohn, not the platform). Blacky had risen to his feet and was watching with the alert readiness of a boar who had been in enough fights to recognize the preliminary stages.

Kalmaris was composing something. He had his waterlogged guitar out and was quietly fingering a chord progression that he would later describe as "Confrontation in B Minor," and which was, in its way, his version of taking notes.

Lufnik stood at parade rest, his posture communicating that he had an opinion about the tactical inadvisability of internal conflict during an active threat scenario but was choosing not to share it. A puddle had formed near his left heel. He did not look down at it.

Milo was about to intervene -- he had prepared a twelve-point proposal for structured investigation that he felt would satisfy both the dwarven emphasis on methodological rigor and the piratical emphasis on getting things done -- when Lamil spoke.

Three words.

"Try the well."

* * *

Everyone looked at the elf. Lamil was standing beside the well at the center of the clearing, his hand resting on its stone rim in the way he rested his hand on trees -- not leaning, but listening. His eyes were closed.

"The well?" Milo said.

Lamil opened his eyes. He looked at the well. He looked at the platform. He did not explain, because Lamil Morgenhauch did not explain. Explanation was, by his apparent philosophy, a concession to people who did not pay sufficient attention to the obvious.

Milo looked at the well. He had tested the water from it on day two -- clean, normal, no contaminants. He had not, he realized, looked at the well *itself*. The structure. The stonework.

He crossed to it and looked down. The well was deep -- deeper than a village this size would need. The walls were stone, the same ancient stone as the platform, and they were covered in the same carvings. The carvings spiraled downward, shrinking with the perspective, descending into a darkness that seemed thicker than simple absence of light.

"Oh," he said.

"Obviously," said Serox, who had arrived beside him and was already examining the stonework with the expression of a man who had known this all along. He had not known this all along. The speed with which he had repositioned from "the platform is the entrance" to "the well is obviously the entrance" was, even by his standards, impressive.

"You said the platform--" Longjohn began.

"I said the acoustic resonance indicated a chamber below grade. The well provides vertical access to a sub-surface space. This is entirely consistent with my assessment."

"Your assessment was about the platform."

"My assessment was about the subsurface chamber. The platform and the well are both surface features of the same underlying structure. As I said."

He had not said this. But the justification was delivered with such absolute conviction that Milo found himself briefly questioning his own memory, which was, he recognized, exactly the kind of cognitive doubt that the island was supposed to be causing. He made a note.

> **From the journal of Milo Quickspark:** *Day 5. The well. The carvings match the platform's -- same period, same style, same builders. Why didn't I examine it sooner? The well is the access point. The platform is the marker. Together they form a single structure: a sealed chamber with a vertical shaft. The shadow's source is below.*
>
> *Interpersonal observation: Lamil identified the connection in three words. I required four pages of notes and failed to see it. Serox required twenty minutes of structural analysis and missed it entirely, though he will never admit this. Recommend further study of druidic observational methodology.*
>
> *Also: Serox's capacity for retrospective self-justification may constitute a hitherto undocumented psychological phenomenon. Considering naming it.*

* * *

Getting down the well required cooperation, which required organization, which required them to agree on who was in charge.

This took longer than it should have.

"I'll go first," said Longjohn, because going first was what you did when you'd spent years being the only person on an island who could check if something was dangerous.

"Negative," said Lufnik. "Point position requires tactical assessment capabilities and combat readiness. I should lead."

"I've survived two shipwrecks, a marooning, and six months of eating whatever I could catch with my hands. I think I can handle a well."

"With respect, survival experience and tactical point operations require different skill sets."

"With respect, I didn't ask."

"I should go first," said Kalmaris, and the reason was not tactical but the same reason Kalmaris did everything: it would be dramatic. The image of the bard descending into darkness, wings spread against the well's narrow walls, lantern in hand -- it would have made an excellent opening to a ballad. "The Descent of Kalmaris" -- yes, he could hear the melody already, something in a haunting minor key--

"You won't fit," Serox said. "Your wingspan exceeds the well diameter by approximately forty centimeters. Obviously."

Kalmaris looked at the well. He looked at his wings. The math was, regrettably, correct.

"I could fold them."

"You could. You would then be unable to catch yourself if you fell. You would also be unable to fly. You would be a bird who cannot fly, falling down a hole. This is not dramatic. This is gravity."

There was, Milo noted, a particular kind of insult that only a dwarf could deliver -- the kind that was not cruel because it was not intended as an insult at all. It was simply an assessment. Serox did not care about Kalmaris's feelings; he cared about wingspan measurements and well diameters. That the accurate measurement of these things wounded the bard's pride was, from Serox's perspective, the bard's problem.

"I'll go first," Milo said.

Everyone looked at him.

"I'm the smallest. I have alchemical light sources. I need to be close to the phenomenon to measure it. And Blobby can scout ahead."

Blobby, who had been sitting on Milo's shoulder in a shade of attentive yellow, wobbled in a way that Milo chose to interpret as eager volunteering. Whether Blobby had, in fact, volunteered for anything was unclear. But the wobble was committed to, and there was no going back now.

"The gnome goes first," Longjohn said, with the tone of a man evaluating this plan against all possible alternatives and finding it acceptable primarily because it was not his problem.

"The gnome has a name," said Milo.

"The gnome goes first," Longjohn repeated, because pirates did not, as a rule, update their phrasing upon request.

* * *

The descent was not pleasant. The well's walls were slick with moisture and age, and the hand-holds carved into the stone were spaced for someone considerably taller than a gnome. Milo managed by wedging himself sideways, pressing his back against one wall and his feet against the other, and inching downward with the graceless determination of a man who knew that dignity was not a factor in scientific discovery.

Blobby went ahead, dropped into the darkness with the boneless confidence of a creature for whom falling was merely a faster form of oozing. He hit the bottom -- somewhere below, in the dark -- with a faint *plop* and turned a vivid, phosphorescent blue that cast the well's lower reaches in an eerie aquatic light.

Milo followed. The others followed Milo, in an order that had been negotiated, renegotiated, and finally settled by Lamil pointing at each of them in sequence and then descending himself without waiting for confirmation. Lamil's approach to leadership was not to argue for authority but to simply start doing things until everyone else fell in line. It was remarkably effective. It was also remarkably irritating, primarily to Serox, who maintained that the descent order should have been determined by weight distribution and structural load calculations.

"The well's load-bearing capacity is not infinite," he said, halfway down, his voice echoing off the wet stone. "If we exceed the lateral pressure tolerances--"

"Then the well collapses and we all die," said Longjohn, from above. "Thank you for that."

"You're welcome. Forewarned is forearmed."

"That's not how that expression is supposed to make people feel."

At the bottom, they found a passage.

It was low -- Kalmaris had to duck, and even Serox, who was not tall, had to lower his head at certain points. The stone was worked, carved with the same spiraling patterns as the well walls, and the air was cool and still and smelled of things that had been underground for a very long time.

Milo held up a vial of alchemical light -- a mixture of phosphorescent compounds suspended in mineral oil, one of his more reliable inventions. The light was steady, cool, and revealed the passage stretching ahead into a darkness that seemed to absorb illumination rather than retreat from it.

"The light doesn't carry as far as it should," he said, frowning at the vial. "The luminous output should provide visibility for approximately fifteen meters in an enclosed space. I'm getting maybe eight."

"The darkness is eating your light," Lamil said.

Milo opened his mouth to provide a scientific rebuttal -- light absorption coefficients, the reflective properties of stone, the potential presence of a particulate medium -- and then closed it. Because the elf was right. The darkness ahead of them had a quality that was not merely an absence of light but a presence of something else. Something that pushed back.

Blacky pressed closer to Longjohn's leg. The boar's ears were flat. His breathing was quick and shallow.

"Easy," Longjohn murmured, his hand finding the coarse bristles behind Blacky's ear. "Easy, boy. We go forward. We see what there is to see. And if it's bad, we leave."

He was talking to the boar. But everyone heard it, and everyone took something from it, and nobody said so. There are moments when the simplest words, spoken to the simplest audience, carry the weight of something larger.

They went forward. Six strangers, a blob, and a boar, walking into a darkness that did not want them there.

It was, from a purely tactical standpoint, inadvisable.

They went anyway.

> **From the journal of Milo Quickspark:** *Day 5, underground. Light absorption anomaly: documented. The darkness ahead is not passive -- it resists illumination. This is inconsistent with all known optical physics. Either I am missing a variable, or the physics in this location are not entirely... reliable.*
>
> *We are going forward. This is not, strictly speaking, a scientific decision. It is a collective one, made by a group of people who have decided to trust each other enough to walk into the dark together.*
>
> *I note this because it is new. Not the darkness. The trust.*
>
> *Both are interesting. The trust, I think, is more so.*

**Author's Note:**

The Serox-Longjohn argument wrote itself the moment I put them in the same scene with a problem to solve. They're both right, which is why they can't agree: Serox is right that understanding the structure matters, and Longjohn is right that speed matters. The fact that Lamil resolved it in three words while they were still arguing is the most Lamil thing I've ever written.

The well descent was fun to choreograph. Six people with six different opinions about who goes first into a dark hole -- it's comedy, but it's also the first time they've had to actually coordinate, and the cracks in their cooperation are the same cracks that will become strengths later. The argument about who leads is also an argument about who they are to each other.

Chapter 6 is where the darkness pushes back. Things are about to get significantly less funny. See you Monday.
