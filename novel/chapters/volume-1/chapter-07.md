# Chapter 7: Hypothesis and Hazard

The plan, such as it was, emerged over breakfast.

Breakfast was coconut, dried fish, and a flatbread that Milo had prepared using a technique he described as "thermally optimized surface dehydration" and that everyone else described as "burned." Blobby ate four pieces and turned an approving shade of amber. Blacky ate three pieces and the leaf they had been served on. The others ate what the villagers offered and left Milo's contributions untouched with the coordinated subtlety of people who had been practicing.

"We know three things," Milo said, brushing charcoal from his notes. "First: the entity beneath the village is feeding on the villagers' cognitive function through shadow extraction. Second: the containment system -- which appears to be a warding framework of considerable age and sophistication -- is failing, accelerating the extraction rate. Third: Lamil's wildfire can repel the shadows, but not indefinitely."

He looked at the group. They looked back at him with expressions ranging from attentive (Lufnik) to skeptical (Longjohn) to deliberately inscrutable (Lamil).

"What we don't know," Milo continued, "is significantly more concerning. We don't know the nature of the entity -- what it is, where it came from, whether it can be destroyed or merely re-contained. We don't know why the containment is failing now, after what appears to be centuries of successful function. And we don't know if there are other factors we haven't identified."

"So we know almost nothing," said Longjohn.

"We know a great deal. We simply don't know enough."

"That's the same thing said longer."

"It is not. Knowing almost nothing implies ignorance. Knowing a great deal but not enough implies a research deficit. The distinction is--"

"Irrelevant to the people upstairs losing their minds."

Milo opened his mouth. Closed it. Longjohn had a way of collapsing elaborate arguments into their functional components, and while Milo found this intellectually frustrating, he could not deny its efficiency.

"Agreed," he said. "So we investigate. Quickly."

> **From the journal of Milo Quickspark:** *Day 6. Proposed investigation plan: divide resources to maximize coverage. Three teams, three objectives. Reconnaissance, historical analysis, and environmental assessment conducted in parallel. Projected timeline: six hours. Actual timeline: subject to the variable cooperation of five individuals with incompatible methodologies and one boar.*
>
> *Noted: Longjohn's capacity for reducing my analysis to its simplest components is both infuriating and, from a purely communicative standpoint, effective. Recommend developing a more concise presentation style. After this investigation.*

* * *

The pairings were Milo's idea. The arguments about the pairings were everyone else's.

"We need someone with knowledge of the local environment with someone who can read tracks and patterns," Milo said. "Lamil and Lufnik -- the forest and the perimeter. If the containment failure has environmental causes, you'll find them."

Lamil nodded. Lufnik said, "Acknowledged." The pairing was accepted with a speed that surprised everyone except Milo, who had observed that the elf and the soldier, despite appearing to have nothing in common, shared a fundamental quality: they both preferred to operate in silence and found the concept of unnecessary conversation physically uncomfortable.

"Longjohn and Serox -- the village infrastructure. The well, the platform, any other structures that might be connected to the containment system. Longjohn reads surfaces. Serox reads structures. Together you should be able to map what's down there without going back down."

Longjohn looked at Serox. Serox looked at Longjohn. The look they exchanged was the visual equivalent of two cats being informed they would be sharing a box.

"I work alone," said Serox.

"We're on an island. You don't have that option."

"Then I work with someone who doesn't interrupt my assessments with nautical metaphors."

"Then I work with someone who doesn't evaluate the load-bearing capacity of a conversation."

"That doesn't even mean anything."

"Neither does half of what you say, but you say it with such confidence that people assume it's important."

"It *is* important."

"Obviously," said Longjohn, and the word landed between them with the specific weight of a man turning another man's verbal tic into a weapon. Serox's eye twitched. Milo made a note.

"And the third pair?" Kalmaris asked, though he already knew.

"You and me. The villagers. Hera and the lucid residents. The containment didn't fail on its own -- something triggered it, or something changed. The history is in their memories, which means we need to talk to the people who still have memories before they don't."

"My dear Milo," Kalmaris said, and his voice had shifted into the register he used when preparing a performance, "interviewing people is what I *do*."

"You sing at people. That's different."

"It is not. A bard listens. A bard observes. A bard draws out the story that people carry but don't know how to tell." He adjusted his feathers. "I am, in fact, the ideal investigative partner."

"You are the remaining investigative partner."

"I choose to hear those as the same thing."

> **DM's Note:** There is a reason that the most effective groups are the ones whose members disagree about everything except the goal. Agreement is comfortable. It is also stagnant. A group that agrees on methodology will pursue a single approach with admirable efficiency and miss everything that approach doesn't cover. A group that disagrees -- passionately, loudly, with the occasional threat of physical violence -- will cover six approaches simultaneously and stumble, arguing all the way, into answers that no single perspective could have found.
>
> Milo understood this instinctively. He understood it the way he understood chemical reactions: two incompatible substances, combined under the right conditions, produce something neither could produce alone. The trick was controlling the reaction. The risk was that sometimes the reaction controlled you.

* * *

Lamil and Lufnik walked into the forest without speaking.

This suited them both. Lamil because speech was, by his reckoning, an inefficient use of energy that could be better spent listening to the world. Lufnik because silence was the natural state of a soldier on reconnaissance, and because silence meant not accidentally saying something that might lead to questions about the puddle that had formed in his boot print three steps back.

He did not look at the boot print. He walked forward. The puddle would evaporate. They always did.

The forest was different from what it had been three days ago. Lamil could feel it in the way the trees held their branches -- tighter, closer to the trunks, the arboreal equivalent of a person folding their arms. The undergrowth was thinner. The birdsong was gone. The insects, which had been enormous and enthusiastic, had retreated to wherever enormous insects go when the environment becomes hostile.

"The border has moved," Lamil said, after twenty minutes of walking.

Lufnik assessed the treeline. "The trees have pulled back?"

"Seven meters since we arrived. Possibly more."

"Rate of retreat?"

"Accelerating."

Lufnik processed this with the efficiency of a man evaluating tactical data. "Correlation with the containment failure?"

"Direct. The trees are responding to what's beneath the village. They're trying to get away from it."

"Trees can't move."

"Trees can do many things that people assume they cannot. Moving is one of them. It simply takes patience."

Lamil placed his hand against a trunk. Closed his eyes. For a long moment, the only sound was the wind in the canopy -- except it wasn't wind, Lufnik realized. It was the trees themselves, shifting, a slow collective motion that was invisible at any given moment but undeniable over time.

"What are they saying?" Lufnik asked, and was immediately embarrassed by the question, because soldiers did not ask trees what they were saying. Except that he was standing in a forest with a druid who demonstrably could talk to trees, and the alternative to asking was remaining ignorant, and ignorance was tactically inadvisable.

Lamil opened his eyes. He looked at Lufnik with an expression that the soldier couldn't quite read -- something between surprise and approval.

"They remember," Lamil said. "There was a dragon. A long time ago. It lived in the shadow between the island and the sun, and when it died, its shadow didn't. The trees remember the day the shadow stayed behind."

"How long ago?"

"Before the trees were trees. When they were seeds. Before that, even." Lamil paused. "The forest's memory is long. It remembers the dragon's shadow falling across the ground for the first time. It felt like the world getting colder."

Lufnik stood very still. Water condensed on the bark of the nearest tree, running downward in thin lines that had no obvious atmospheric cause. He did not notice. Lamil did.

The elf looked at the water on the bark. Looked at Lufnik. Said nothing.

But his sentence, when it came, was four words longer than it needed to be: "Thank you for asking that."

* * *

Longjohn and Serox argued their way around the village perimeter in a manner that was, depending on your perspective, either completely unproductive or enormously efficient.

Serox knelt at every stone, every wall, every structural element that might be connected to the underground chamber, and delivered assessments with the rapid-fire certainty of an auctioneer selling facts. "This foundation predates the village by at least three centuries. The mortar composition is consistent with the chamber walls. This is part of the containment infrastructure -- load-bearing, not decorative."

Longjohn, walking beside him, read the ground. "Three sets of tracks here. Regular traffic. Someone's been checking this wall -- see the wear pattern on the mud? Kneeling, leaning, pressing their hand here. Same position every time."

"That's irrelevant. The structural analysis is--"

"Not irrelevant. Someone in this village knows about the containment. Someone's been maintaining it." He pointed to a spot where the mortar between stones had been recently filled -- a different color, a different texture, newer than the surrounding work. "See that? Patch job. Recent. Whoever did this knew what they were doing."

Serox looked at the mortar. His eyes narrowed with the specific intensity of a craftsman encountering another craftsman's work.

"Adequate," he said.

This was, Longjohn was beginning to understand, as close to praise as the dwarf would ever come.

"So who?" Longjohn asked.

"The village chief. Obviously. The competence is consistent."

It was not obvious. But in this case, Serox was probably right.

* * *

Milo and Kalmaris found Hera mending fishing nets in the village square. She was doing it with the mechanical efficiency of a woman whose hands had memorized the work so thoroughly that her mind was free to think about other things. Given the state of the village, the other things were considerable.

"We know about the chamber," Milo said, because directness was the only approach his personality permitted. "Beneath the well. The containment. The shadow."

Hera's hands did not stop moving. Her face did not change. But something behind her eyes shifted -- a door opening, or closing, or both.

"You went down there," she said. It was not a question.

"Yes."

"And you came back."

"Also yes."

"Most don't." She set the net aside. "How much do you know?"

Milo told her. He told her precisely, completely, and with the clinical vocabulary of a man who found safety in accuracy. Hera listened without interrupting, which Milo appreciated, and without visible surprise, which Milo found more informative than any of her answers.

When he finished, she said: "My grandmother built the most recent layer of wards. Her grandmother built the layer before that. The village has been maintaining the containment for as long as the village has existed. We settled here to watch the pit. Not because this was a good place to live."

"The containment is failing," Milo said.

"The containment has been failing for twenty years. The shadow is stronger than it was. The wards weaken faster than we can repair them. My grandmother could hold the seals for a decade with a single ritual. I can hold them for a month, if I don't sleep."

"And the people? The forgetting?"

Hera's hands, which had been steady throughout, trembled once. "That started eight months ago. The first was my brother."

Kalmaris, who had been uncharacteristically quiet -- listening, as he had claimed bards do -- spoke for the first time. His voice was gentle in a way that stripped it of all performance.

"Is he one of the ones we've seen? The ones who've..." He did not finish the sentence. He did not need to.

"He mends nets that don't need mending," Hera said. "He was the best fisherman on the island. Now he mends nets that don't have holes, and he doesn't know my name."

The silence that followed was not uncomfortable. It was the silence of people sitting with someone else's grief, giving it the space it required.

Kalmaris, very quietly, began to play. Not a song -- just a progression of chords, soft and slow, the kind of music that exists to fill a silence without breaking it. His guitar was still waterlogged and half the strings were flat, and the sound that emerged was imperfect and rough and exactly right.

Hera listened. After a moment, her hands returned to the net, and her fingers moved in time with the music, and something in her shoulders loosened by a fraction.

Milo watched this and wrote nothing. There were some things that did not belong in a journal.

> **From the journal of Milo Quickspark:** *Day 6, midday. Investigation results compiled. Lamil/Lufnik team: the entity is the residual shadow of a dragon, left behind when the creature died. It has been feeding and growing for centuries. The forest remembers and is retreating. Longjohn/Serox team: the containment infrastructure extends throughout the village. Someone (confirmed: Hera) has been maintaining it. Recent repairs insufficient. Milo/Kalmaris team: the village was founded specifically to guard the containment. The shadow's growth has outpaced the wardens' capacity. Timeline is months, not years.*
>
> *Note: Kalmaris played music for the village chief. This was not part of the investigation protocol. It was, however, the most useful thing anyone did all morning.*
>
> *I am beginning to suspect that the variables I am not measuring are more important than the ones I am.*

The pairs tell you everything. And Milo watches Kalmaris play music for a grieving woman and realizes, for the first time, that his notebook might be missing the point.
