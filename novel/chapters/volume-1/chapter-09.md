# Chapter 9: The Bard's Reflection

Kalmaris had stopped looking at reflective surfaces.

He did not announce this. He did not make a dramatic declaration about the state of mirrors in general or his relationship to them in particular. He simply... stopped. When the group passed the water troughs, he looked elsewhere. When Milo's vials caught the light, he turned his head. When rain collected in the broad leaves of the canopy trees and formed small, still pools that reflected the sky with treacherous clarity, Kalmaris found something else to study -- a beetle, a cloud, the structural composition of his own feathers, which he examined with the close attention of a man who wanted to look at anything that wasn't his own face.

Nobody noticed. Or rather, nobody commented. Longjohn noticed, because Longjohn noticed everything and said almost nothing, which was the inverse of Kalmaris's approach to life and possibly why they got along.

On the eighth morning, Kalmaris went to the stream.

He went alone, which was unusual. Kalmaris was not, by temperament or preference, a solitary creature. He was a performer, and performers require audiences the way fire requires oxygen -- not as a luxury but as a condition of existence. A Kalmaris without an audience was a diminished thing, a stage without lights, and he avoided it whenever possible.

But the stream was where the water was, and water was necessary, and the pool at the base of the cascade was the clearest water on the island, and Kalmaris was thirsty.

He knelt at the pool's edge and cupped water in his hands and drank. He did not look down. He kept his eyes on the cascade, on the rocks, on the ferns growing from the cliff face. He did not look at the pool.

The pool looked at him anyway.

> **DM's Note:** There is a particular kind of courage that goes unrecognized because it looks, from the outside, like a person simply doing an ordinary thing. Kneeling by a stream. Drinking water. These are not heroic acts. But for a man who has seen his reflection move independently, who has watched a mirror crack along the line of his own face, who lies awake at night wondering if the thing looking back at him from every polished surface is really him -- for that man, kneeling at a pool of clear water requires a bravery that has nothing to do with swords or monsters.
>
> Kalmaris did not think of himself as brave. He thought of himself as dramatic, which is different. Drama is the art of making large things look larger. Bravery is the art of making terrifying things look small enough to survive. Kalmaris was about to need both.

His reflection was perfect. Every feather in place. The crest at the correct angle. The eyes the right color -- warm amber, sharp and intelligent, the eyes of a man who had seen the world from both above and below and preferred above.

He finished drinking. He stood up. He turned to go.

His reflection didn't.

It stayed. Kneeling at the pool's edge, hands still cupped, head still bowed. For three full seconds -- Milo would later establish this through careful reconstruction -- the reflection remained in the water after Kalmaris had risen and stepped away.

Then it looked up.

Not at the sky. Not at the cliff face or the cascade or the ferns. It looked *out of the pool*, directly at the space where Kalmaris was standing, and for a moment its eyes were not amber. They were dark. Not brown-dark or shadow-dark but the particular darkness of something that had depth where depth should not be -- a well with no bottom, a sky with no stars.

Kalmaris was three steps from the pool when it happened, and he did not see it, because he had already turned away, because he was very carefully not looking at reflective surfaces, because the last time he had looked at his own reflection it had cracked.

The pool rippled. The reflection dissolved. The water settled back into clarity, reflecting nothing but sky and stone and the normal, empty world.

But the water retained something. A darkness, very faint, at the bottom of the pool -- a shadow where no shadow should have been, cast by nothing visible, pointed toward nothing in particular.

It would be there tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that. Growing, slowly, in the way that things grow when they are patient and very, very sure of what they want.

* * *

Milo, unaware of events at the stream, was engaged in what he considered his second-most important experiment of the week.

He was cooking.

"The key," he explained to Blobby, who was perched on a rock and observing with the color-shifting attention of a creature who had learned that Milo's cooking, while catastrophic to every other palate, was reliably excellent from his perspective, "is in the binding agent. Standard tropical cuisine relies on coconut oil or animal fat as the primary lipid matrix. I am substituting a compound of my own design -- a blend of mineral salts, pulverized fire coral, and a small quantity of the sulphuric paste I use for cleaning alchemical glassware."

Blobby turned a shade of excited tangerine.

"Exactly. The sulphuric base should provide a unique flavor profile while also -- and this is the innovative part -- serving as a mild preservative. The meal should be edible for up to three days after preparation, assuming ambient temperature remains below thirty degrees."

It was a stew. Of course it was a stew. Milo's approach to cooking was, like his approach to alchemy, iterative: he started with a base formula and modified it until the result was either perfect or explosive, with a distribution that skewed heavily toward the latter.

He served it to the villagers.

This was an act of genuine kindness. The lucid villagers were exhausted -- they had been maintaining the containment, caring for their affected neighbors, and living under siege from an enemy they couldn't see or fight. Hot food, prepared by someone else, was a gift. That the gift tasted like hot rocks dissolved in seawater was, from their perspective, a secondary consideration. They ate it because someone had made it for them, and that mattered more than flavor.

They ate it, and they said thank you, and Milo glowed with the particular satisfaction of a man who had helped and had no idea that his help had been endured rather than enjoyed.

The leftover stew sat in the pot, forgotten, beside the cookfire.

The vine creature came at dusk.

> **DM's Note:** The island of Moreva, it should be understood, contained more than one problem. The shadow beneath the village was the largest and most immediate problem, but the jungle surrounding the village harbored its own collection of hazards -- creatures that had adapted to the island's unusual environment in ways that defied comfortable classification. The vine creatures were one such hazard: semi-motile plant organisms that moved through the undergrowth with the slow, deliberate patience of something that did not need to chase its prey because its prey, eventually, came to it.
>
> They were not, strictly speaking, dangerous. They were inconvenient. They grasped, they constricted, they dragged things toward the jungle floor with the methodical persistence of a bureaucracy processing paperwork. They had never, in the village's recorded history, killed anyone. They had, however, ruined several picnics.

The vine creature emerged from the treeline with the unhurried confidence of a plant that had never encountered resistance. It was approximately four meters long, thick as Serox's arm, and covered in fine thorns that served no obvious purpose beyond making it unpleasant to hold. It moved across the ground toward the village center, feeling its way by vibration, and its path took it directly past Milo's unattended cookpot.

The vine touched the pot. The vine entered the pot. The vine, presumably operating on whatever passed for curiosity in a semi-motile plant, sampled the stew.

What happened next was rapid and, from a botanical standpoint, unprecedented.

The vine recoiled. Not slowly, not with the measured retreat of a plant encountering an unfavorable stimulus, but with the full-body convulsion of an organism that had encountered something fundamentally incompatible with its continued existence. It whipped backward, shedding thorns, its surface blistering where the stew had made contact. It retreated into the jungle at a speed that Milo would later describe as "approximately three times faster than any recorded velocity for organisms of the genus Ambulovitis" and that Longjohn would describe as "like it had somewhere to be."

The pot was undisturbed. The stew was undisturbed. A faint wisp of acrid smoke rose from the spot where the vine had touched the surface, suggesting that Milo's "mild preservative" was performing additional, unintended functions.

Milo arrived at the scene moments later, drawn by the commotion, and assessed the situation with the rapid pattern-recognition of a man whose experiments frequently produced unexpected results.

"Fascinating," he said, examining the blistered thorns the vine had shed. "The sulphuric compound in the binding agent appears to have a phytotoxic effect on the local vine fauna. The reaction suggests an acid-base incompatibility at the cellular level, causing rapid tissue necrosis on contact."

"Your stew poisoned a plant," Longjohn translated.

"My stew demonstrated a previously unknown application as a botanical deterrent."

"Your stew. Poisoned. A plant."

"That is a reductive--"

"The plant that eats everything. That plant ate your stew and ran away."

Milo opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

"I'm going to need more of the sulphuric paste," he said.

> **From the journal of Milo Quickspark:** *Day 8. Unexpected discovery: the sulphuric binding compound I developed for the village stew demonstrates significant phytotoxic properties against local vine fauna (genus Ambulovitis, motile variant). Contact produced immediate tissue necrosis, blistering, and rapid withdrawal response.*
>
> *Potential applications: perimeter defense, botanical specimen collection, pest control.*
>
> *Note: Longjohn described this as "your stew poisoned a plant." While technically accurate, this framing fails to capture the scientific significance of the discovery. The compound's reactivity suggests a broader spectrum of biological interactions worth investigating.*
>
> *Secondary note: the villagers ate this stew. They appear unharmed. I attribute this to the significant difference between human and plant cellular chemistry. Also, they may simply be polite.*
>
> *Tertiary note: Blobby ate six servings. Blobby is also unharmed. Blobby may not be entirely organic.*

* * *

Kalmaris returned from the stream that evening looking like a man who had fought something and wasn't sure if he'd won. He was quieter than usual -- not silent, because Kalmaris silent was alarming in a way that demanded attention, but muted. Reduced. As though someone had turned down the volume on his entire personality.

He sat by the fire and tuned his guitar with mechanical precision, tightening and loosening strings in patterns that had no musical purpose, his fingers busy while his mind was elsewhere.

"The stream is clear," he said, when Milo asked where he'd been. "Good water. Nothing unusual."

It was not a lie, exactly. It was just not the truth.

Longjohn, sitting across the fire with Blacky's head in his lap, watched Kalmaris tune the same string for the fourth time and said nothing. But his eyes tracked the bard with the particular attention of a man who recognized the specific body language of someone carrying a secret they weren't ready to share.

He recognized it because he saw it every time he looked in a mirror himself. The difference was that his secret sat beside him, breathing and warm and occasionally eating things that weren't food.

The difference was that his secret had a heartbeat.

"Play something," Longjohn said. It was not a request. It was an offering -- the gift of purpose from one man who understood purposelessness to another.

Kalmaris looked at him. Something passed between them -- not understanding, not quite, but recognition. The acknowledgment of one closed door by another.

He played. Something slow and wandering, a melody that moved the way water moves over stones -- finding its path not by force but by patience. The notes were imperfect. The guitar was still damaged, still salt-warped, still missing a string that Kalmaris had replaced with a length of fishing line that Hera had given him. But imperfect music, played with real feeling, has a quality that perfection cannot replicate. It sounds like the truth.

Around the fire, the others listened. Serox closed his eyes, which was as close to appreciation as his face would permit. Lamil's hand, resting on the ground, pressed flat against the earth. Lufnik sat very still, his water skin untouched beside him, and the water inside it did not ripple.

Milo wrote in his journal: *Kalmaris played tonight. Something changed in his demeanor today -- post-stream visit, observable reduction in vocal energy, increased repetitive behavior (guitar tuning). Possible cause: fatigue, stress, or undisclosed event. Monitoring.*

He did not write that the music was beautiful. He did not need to. Some things, even in a journal, are self-evident.

**Author's Note:**

Two things happened in this chapter that readers might miss on first pass. First: Kalmaris's reflection stayed in the pool for three seconds. Three seconds doesn't sound like much. But his mirror in Chapter 4 was a fraction of a second. The escalation is geometric, not linear. Whatever is in the reflection is getting stronger, and it's getting stronger at a pace that should worry everyone.

Second: Milo's stew poisoned a vine creature. This is a comedy beat, and it's meant to make you laugh, and I hope it does. But file it away. The idea that Milo's cooking is so terrible it functions as a weapon is going to come back, and when it does, you'll remember the vine creature and think: oh. He told us.

Chapter 10 is about trust, and what it costs, and what happens when a soldier's secret almost doesn't stay secret. See you Wednesday.
