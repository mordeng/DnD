# Chapter 10: A Brief Treatise on Trust

The girl was seven years old and she was drowning.

This was not, in the strictest sense, accurate. She was not in water. She was standing in the village square, in broad daylight, surrounded by people who could see her. But the shadow that had wrapped itself around her ankles was pulling her downward -- not physically, not into the ground, but *into herself* -- and the sound she made was the sound of someone going under.

Her name was Lira. She was Hera's niece. She had, until forty seconds ago, been one of the last children in Moreva still fully lucid, and her lucidity was leaving her in real-time, visible to everyone, stoppable by no one.

Milo saw it first. He was crossing the square with a tray of samples -- soil, stone, water, shadow-touched vegetation -- when the girl's shadow detached from her feet and began to move independently. He had documented shadow detachment before. This was different. The shadow was not sliding away. It was wrapping *upward*, climbing her legs like dark ivy, and where it touched, her body went slack.

"Lufnik!" Milo shouted, because Lufnik was nearest, and because in a crisis the mind reaches for the person most likely to act without hesitation.

Lufnik was already moving. He had assessed the situation in the time it took Milo to form the first syllable of his name -- threat identification, proximity analysis, available resources, response options. He crossed the square in four strides. His hand closed around the girl's arm and he pulled.

The shadow pulled back.

It was not a physical contest. There was no resistance Lufnik could feel in his muscles, no weight he could counter with strength. The shadow's grip was not on the girl's body but on something deeper, something that could not be grasped with hands. He pulled and she moved toward him and the shadow stretched but did not break, and the girl's eyes were open and empty and looking at something that was not the village square.

"Come on," Lufnik said. His voice was controlled. His hands were steady. Everything about him was exactly what training and discipline demanded.

But the water in the well behind him began to rise.

It climbed the stone walls of the well shaft -- silently, against gravity, with the smooth inevitability of something responding to a signal only it could hear. It crested the rim and spilled across the packed earth, running in channels that had nothing to do with the slope of the ground, converging on the place where Lufnik stood with the girl in his arms.

The water reached the shadow. And the shadow, which had been impervious to physical force, which had resisted Lufnik's grip and Milo's light and everything else they had thrown at it -- the shadow *flinched*.

The water was not warm and it was not bright and it should not have affected a shadow in any measurable way. But it did. The shadow retracted from the water's edge the way the vine creature had retracted from Milo's stew -- involuntarily, as though touching something that was fundamentally incompatible with its nature.

The girl gasped. Her eyes cleared. She looked up at Lufnik with the bewildered focus of someone surfacing from deep water, and said, "Where did the dark go?"

"Away," Lufnik said. "It went away."

The water retreated. It slid back across the ground, back up the well shaft, back down to where it belonged. It took approximately four seconds, and it moved with the self-conscious haste of something trying to pretend it had never left.

Lufnik set the girl down. He stepped away. He did not look at the well.

>
> He had died. He had prayed -- not with faith, not with devotion, but with the raw, unformed desperation of a man who wanted one more breath. The moon had answered. He did not understand why. He did not understand the power it had left in him, or what it meant, or what it would cost. He understood only that the water responded to him, and that the response was visible, and that visible things invite questions, and that questions lead to answers he was not prepared to give.
>
> The girl was safe. The shadow was gone. The water had returned to the well. And Lufnik Springveil was standing in the village square, heart hammering, hoping -- with the same desperate intensity with which he had once prayed to the moon -- that nobody had seen.

* * *

Milo had seen.

Not the water rising from the well -- he had been too focused on the girl to notice the well behind Lufnik. But he had seen the shadow retract. He had seen the water on the ground. And he had observed that the water's retreat pattern was inconsistent with natural drainage.

"Localized atmospheric anomaly," he wrote in his journal that afternoon, frowning at the words as though they were an answer rather than a placeholder. "The water displacement in the village square correlates temporally with the shadow retraction event. Possible explanations: (1) underground pressure fluctuation causing well overflow, (2) seismic activity affecting the water table, (3) shadow retraction creating a localized vacuum that drew water from the nearest source."

He underlined option three. He stared at it. He crossed it out.

He wrote, very small, in the margin: *Or (4) something I'm not seeing.*

> **From the journal of Milo Quickspark:** *Day 8, afternoon. Shadow attack on minor (Lira, age 7, niece of Chief Hera). Intervention by Lufnik Springveil -- physical extraction of subject from shadow contact. Shadow retracted upon contact with well water that had, through means I have not yet determined, overflowed its containment and spread across the ground.*
>
> *The water's behavior was anomalous. Wells do not overflow without pressure changes. There were no detectable seismic events. The water moved toward the shadow contact point with directional specificity that suggests -- but I cannot confirm -- external direction.*
>
> *I have observed 23 instances of unexplained water behavior in Lufnik's proximity. I have attributed each to environmental factors. The statistical probability of 23 independent environmental explanations for 23 spatially correlated events is...*
>
> *...low.*
>
> *I am choosing not to pursue this line of inquiry at present. Lufnik did not wish to be observed. This was evident in his posture, his immediate withdrawal from the scene, and his refusal to discuss the incident afterward. A man who saves a child and then retreats from acknowledgment is not hiding something dangerous. He is hiding something personal.*
>
> *I will respect this. For now.*

This was, in its way, the most significant thing Milo had ever written. Not because of its content, but because of what it represented: a gnome who measured everything choosing, deliberately, not to measure something. Not because the measurement was impossible, but because taking it would breach a trust he valued more than data.

It was the first time in his professional life that Milo Quickspark had prioritized a relationship over a research question, and he had done it so naturally that he barely noticed.

* * *

Lufnik found Milo that evening, after dinner, at the edge of the camp where the gnome was arranging vials and muttering about molecular structures.

"The girl," Lufnik said. "Lira. Status?"

"Stable. Hera is monitoring her. The shadow contact was brief -- she seems to have retained full cognitive function. No signs of the progressive deterioration we've observed in the long-term affected."

"Good." Lufnik paused. Parade rest. Eyes forward. The posture of a man organizing his words the way he organized his equipment: precisely, efficiently, and with no room for unnecessary items. "I want to discuss the incident."

Milo set down a vial. "All right."

"You saw something."

It was not a question. Milo treated it as one anyway, because the fiction of a question gave Lufnik the option of denial, and options mattered when a man was standing at the edge of a disclosure he wasn't ready to make.

"I saw a shadow retract from a child, and I saw water on the ground afterward. The rest is speculation, and I don't speculate without data."

It was a lie. Milo speculated constantly. He speculated recreationally. He had, in his journal, an entire section devoted to speculative hypotheses that began with "What if" and ended with exclamation points. But this particular lie was a gift, and both of them knew it.

Lufnik's jaw tightened. Released. He nodded once.

"Understood," he said. Then, after a pause that was fractionally too long: "Thank you."

He turned to go. Stopped. Turned back.

"The water," he said. "It's not-- I don't--" He stopped. Started again. The military syntax, which usually armored his speech against uncertainty, was failing him. "There are aspects of my situation that are not fully understood. Even by me. The water is... a symptom. Of something that happened. I am managing it."

"I know," said Milo.

"You know?"

"I know that you're managing something. I don't know what. I don't need to." He picked up the vial again. Held it up to the firelight. Examined the compound inside with the studious attention of a man who was giving another man privacy by looking at something else. "When you decide you want to explain, I'll listen. Until then, I have twenty-three instances of anomalous water behavior attributed to environmental factors, and I'm perfectly content to continue attributing them to environmental factors."

"Twenty-three?"

"I'm thorough."

A sound escaped Lufnik that was not quite a laugh. It was too controlled for a laugh, too clipped, too surprised by its own existence. But it was in the neighborhood of laughter, and from Lufnik Springveil, it was the equivalent of a standing ovation.

"Noted," he said, and left.

Behind him, a small puddle evaporated from the ground where he'd been standing. Milo watched it go and wrote nothing.

* * *

That night, the moon was full.

It hung over Moreva like a lantern -- bright, silver-white, closer than it had any right to be. The kind of moon that makes poets write bad verse and wolves make noise and ordinary people look up from their ordinary lives and feel, just for a moment, that the world is larger and stranger than their daily experience suggests.

Lufnik stood at the edge of the camp and looked at it.

He did not pray. He had prayed once, dying, and had been answered, and the answer had been so vast and so incomprehensible that he had spent every day since trying to pretend it had never happened. But the moon was full tonight, and when the moon was full, the water in him responded -- a deep, tidal pull, like the ocean answering a gravity it could not resist.

The dew on the grass around his feet gathered into droplets. The droplets rolled toward him, slowly, drawn by something that was not wind and not slope and not any force that Lufnik could name or control. They pooled around his boots -- small, quiet, patient.

He stood very still and let them.

From across the camp, Lamil watched. His eyes were open and his body was still and the expression on his face was not surprise, because Lamil had known about the water since the forest -- since the droplets on the bark, since Lufnik had asked what the trees were saying and the water around him had responded to a question he hadn't realized he was asking.

Lamil watched, and said nothing, and let the secret keep itself for a little longer.

> **From the journal of Milo Quickspark:** *Day 8, late. Full moon tonight. Atmospheric conditions clear. Ambient temperature holding at 24 degrees.*
>
> *No anomalous water behavior observed.*
>
> *I did not go looking.*

**Author's Note:**

This is the chapter I'm most proud of so far. Milo choosing not to investigate -- a gnome who measures everything deciding that some things are more important than measurement -- is, I think, the moment where his character arc actually begins. Not when he makes a scientific breakthrough. Not when he solves a puzzle. When he looks at a friend's secret and decides to let it be.

Lufnik's near-reveal is the tightest rope I've had to walk. Too much, and the secret loses power. Too little, and the reader doesn't feel the weight of what he's carrying. The conversation between him and Milo -- two men talking around the thing they're both aware of -- is my favorite scene I've written. Neither of them says the truth, and both of them hear it.

Chapter 11 goes underground again. The cave network is bigger than anyone expected, and the murals on the walls tell a story that Milo really wishes they didn't. See you Friday.
