# Adaptation Notes: D&D Campaign to Novel

This document tracks how campaign events are adapted for the novel. The campaign provides character seeds, world, and key events. The novel gets its own narrative arc designed for readers, not players.

---

## Guiding Principles

1. **The campaign is source material, not scripture.** Events that work at a game table don't always work in prose. Adapt freely.
2. **Character over plot.** If a campaign event doesn't serve a character's arc, cut it or reshape it.
3. **Earn everything.** In D&D, a boss fight can happen because the DM placed it there. In a novel, every confrontation needs narrative setup and emotional stakes.
4. **No D&D mechanics.** No spell slots, levels, AC, hit points, or dice. Magic is thematic, not systematic in the game-mechanic sense.
5. **Combat is emotional, not tactical.** What's at stake matters more than blow-by-blow action. "Blacky charged the elemental" hits harder than detailed combat rounds.

## Key Divergences

### 1. Milo Stays with the Group in Volume 1
**Campaign**: Milo leaves the party early to join the RR-Guild research project. The party adventures without him through arcs 1-2.
**Novel**: Milo is present throughout Volume 1. His separation is the Volume 1 CLIFFHANGER -- he's kidnapped at the end, not the beginning.
**Reason**: Milo is the heart of the group dynamic and a natural focal point for the omniscient narrator. His presence makes his kidnapping more impactful -- the reader has spent 30 chapters watching him before he's taken. In Volume 2, the omniscient narrator follows both the party and Milo's captivity without any POV limitations.

### 2. Shadow Dragon as a Mystery Arc
**Campaign**: The shadow dragon on Moreva is a boss fight.
**Novel**: The shadow dragon is a proper mystery. Clues are planted from Chapter 3. The dragon isn't revealed until Chapter 14. The battle in Chapter 15 is the payoff of investigation, not a random encounter.
**Reason**: Mystery structure creates tension and allows Milo's investigative nature to drive the plot.

### 3. Blacky's Death as Volume 1 Emotional Climax
**Campaign**: Blacky dies in the fire elemental fight -- a combat casualty.
**Novel**: Blacky's death is the emotional climax of Volume 1, Chapter 27. It's set up across 25 chapters of Longjohn-Blacky bonding. The death is a sacrifice, not a casualty. Longjohn's subsequent grief drives his entire Volume 2-3 arc.
**Reason**: Earned emotional weight requires setup. The death of a companion should devastate the reader, not just the character.

### 4. The Auction as a Multi-Chapter Heist
**Campaign**: The auction is a session event with bidding and a fight.
**Novel**: The auction is a 7-chapter arc (V2, Chapters 18-24) with heist/infiltration structure. Each character has a role. The preparation, execution, and consequences are fully dramatized.
**Reason**: The auction is a natural set piece for ensemble fiction. It allows every character to shine in their specialty.

### 5. Doppelganger Established Earlier
**Campaign**: The doppelganger "Milo" is revealed during the boss fight.
**Novel**: Blobby's behavior changes start subtly around V2 Chapter 21. The reader should feel something is wrong before the reveal. Milo's captivity chapters show the doppelganger's creation.
**Reason**: Dramatic irony (reader knows more than characters) creates tension that surprise alone can't match.

### 6. Ratgeber as In-Universe Document
**Campaign**: `ratgeber.md` is a DM reference document written in humorous style.
**Novel**: Milo's "Somewhat Unreliable Guide to Avernus" is an actual document he writes during the Volume 3 journey. Excerpts open every V3 chapter as blue boxes. The Guide is already written in an Adams-adjacent style -- it translates directly.
**Reason**: The guide is already perfect for this purpose. It provides worldbuilding, comedy, and character voice in one device.

### 7. Tiamat Reveal as Planted-Clue Sequence
**Campaign**: Tiamat is a known entity in Avernus (from Descent into Avernus module).
**Novel**: Tiamat is a two-chapter reveal built from clues planted across Volume 3. Five colors mentioned in different contexts. Five shadows. Five rings. When Milo puts it together, the reader should have the pieces to solve it just before he does.
**Reason**: Sanderson's First Law -- a well-foreshadowed revelation is more satisfying than a sudden one. The reader should feel clever, then terrified.

### 8. No D&D Mechanics
**Campaign**: Characters have levels, spell slots, hit dice, AC, etc.
**Novel**: Magic follows thematic systems with narrative costs:
- Alchemy = reagents + ratios + physical toll
- Soul coins = currency that screams + moral weight
- Wildfire = intent-based + corrupted in Hell
- Moon magic = lunar dependency + emotional leakage
- Gargauth = use-equals-growth corruption
**Reason**: Game mechanics break immersion in prose. Thematic systems create dramatic tension.

### 9. Combat is Emotional, Not Tactical
**Campaign**: Combat involves initiative, attack rolls, damage, positioning.
**Novel**: Combat focuses on what's at stake, who's in danger, and what choices characters make under pressure. A fight scene should read like a character scene that happens to involve violence.
**Reason**: Readers care about people, not mechanics. "Blacky charged the elemental to save Longjohn" is a sentence about love. "Blacky rolled 18 on his attack" is not.

### 10. Personal Quests Resolve with Cost
**Campaign**: Character quests have multiple possible outcomes, some better than others.
**Novel**: Every personal quest resolution costs something. There are no free wins. Kalmaris loses power or identity. Lufnik keeps faith or gains ability. Serox keeps integrity or gets the perfect armor. Longjohn saves Blacky or learns to let go.
**Reason**: Meaningful choice requires sacrifice. A hero who gets everything they want for free isn't a hero -- they're a wish-fulfillment device.

## Events NOT Adapted

Some campaign events are cut or significantly reduced:

- **Random encounters** without narrative purpose
- **Shopping/equipment management** (except when it serves character or worldbuilding)
- **Rule disputes or table-talk moments** (no breaking the fourth wall)
- **Events that only happened because of dice rolls** (adapt the outcome, not the mechanism)
- **Duplicate plot beats** (if two events serve the same narrative purpose, merge them)
