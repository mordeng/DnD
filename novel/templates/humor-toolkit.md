# Humor Toolkit

Three humor traditions blended in specific ratios depending on arc position. This document defines when and how to use each.

---

## Douglas Adams -- The Cosmic Absurdist

### When to Use
- Worldbuilding and environmental description
- Cosmic-scale events treated with bureaucratic calm
- Institutional absurdity (guilds, governments, Hell's hierarchy)
- Blue-boxed Guide entries
- Moments where the universe's indifference is the joke

### Techniques
- **Deadpan catastrophe**: Describe terrible things in the tone of a mildly inconvenienced travel writer
- **Scale comedy**: The vastness of a problem makes it funny (the Tarrasque is too large to be scary -- it's a natural disaster with legs)
- **Bureaucratic horror**: Hell has paperwork. The Wandering Emporium has a returns policy. Zariel files reports.
- **The anti-climax**: Build tension, then deflate it with mundane reality
- **Worldbuilding-as-punchline**: The Guide entry format -- informative, authoritative, and deeply unhelpful

### Example
> The Wandering Emporium's return policy was, technically, quite generous. Items could be returned within thirty days for a full refund, provided the item was in its original condition, the receipt was presented, and the customer was still alive. The third condition, Mahadi explained with a smile that contained far too many teeth, was "merely a formality."

### Pitfalls to Avoid
- Don't let absurdism undermine genuine stakes (Adams himself struggled with this in later books)
- Don't apply Adams humor to character moments -- it creates distance when you need intimacy
- Don't overuse the Guide format -- 2-3 per chapter maximum, and only in Volume 3

## Terry Pratchett -- The Warm Satirist

### When to Use
- Character interactions and dialogue
- Footnotes/blue boxes that reveal character
- Institutional satire with a human heart
- Moments where the joke IS the truth (humor as insight)
- Anytime you need warmth alongside wit

### Techniques
- **The footnote aside**: A tangential observation that's funnier than the main text (Milo's alchemist's notes serve this function)
- **Character-voice comedy**: Humor emerging from WHO the person is, not from the situation. Serox's refusal to be wrong. Kalmaris's name rage. Lufnik's puddles.
- **Warm irony**: The narrator sees the absurdity but loves the people in it. These characters are ridiculous and irreplaceable, and the narration knows it.
- **The observed truth**: A joke that makes the reader think "that's exactly right" (Pratchett's hallmark)
- **Running gags with evolution**: A joke that changes meaning over time (Milo's cooking starts as comedy, becomes touching in the V3 finale)

### Example
> Serox examined the wall with the careful attention of a structural engineer assessing a condemned building. "The load-bearing capacity is insufficient," he declared, moments before punching through it. "As I suspected."
>
> Nobody pointed out that he had just confirmed his assessment by creating the very failure he'd predicted. This was not, as Longjohn had once observed, a productive line of conversation.

### Pitfalls to Avoid
- Don't mistake snark for Pratchett. Pratchett's humor is NEVER cruel -- it's warm even when it's sharp
- Don't let footnotes become a crutch -- they should enhance, not replace, the main narrative
- Don't use Pratchett-style humor during climactic moments -- it works in setup and denouement, not at peak tension

## Brandon Sanderson -- The Earned Payoff

### When to Use
- Magic system reveals and clever applications
- Climactic moments where planted details pay off
- Escalation sequences where stakes compound
- Character moments that resolve long arcs
- Any time "surprise that feels inevitable" is the goal

### Techniques
- **The planted detail**: Introduce something early (a rule, an object, a behavior). Let the reader forget it. Bring it back when it matters most.
- **Clever application**: A character uses an established rule in a new way. The reader should be able to look back and see why it works.
- **Escalation through convergence**: Multiple plot threads reach crisis simultaneously. The character must solve them with the tools they have.
- **Earned emotional payoff**: A character's arc reaches resolution in a way that's surprising but retrospectively inevitable.
- **The "aha" moment**: Both the character and the reader realize something at the same time (or the reader realizes just before).

### Example
> I had been documenting the puddles for weeks. Localized atmospheric condensation, I'd written in my notes. Subterranean moisture seepage. Possible alchemical runoff from my own experiments. I had seventeen hypotheses, each more elaborate than the last, and not one of them involved the possibility that my friend was secretly a water mage who couldn't control his powers when he felt things too strongly.
>
> In my defense, "my companion is involuntarily generating water through emotional resonance" is not typically among the first explanations a scientist considers.
>
> In retrospect, it should have been.

### Pitfalls to Avoid
- Don't plant so many details that the reader is overwhelmed -- 3-5 major plants per volume is enough
- Don't explain the payoff -- let the reader feel the satisfaction of connection
- Don't use Sanderson-style reveals for comedy -- they work for drama and wonder, not laughs

## Humor Density by Arc Position

| Position | Adams | Pratchett | Sanderson | Total Humor | Notes |
|----------|-------|-----------|-----------|-------------|-------|
| **Setup** (Ch 1-5) | HIGH | HIGH | Low | Dense | Establish the funny voice early |
| **Rising** (Ch 6-15) | Medium | HIGH | Growing | Balanced | Humor earns trust for serious moments |
| **Pre-Midpoint** (Ch 12-14) | Medium | Medium | Medium | Balanced | Equal blend |
| **Midpoint** (Ch 15-16) | Low | Medium | HIGH | Reduced | Payoffs start landing |
| **Escalation** (Ch 17-24) | Low | Medium | HIGH | Reduced | Stakes compress humor space |
| **Pre-Climax** (Ch 25-27) | Minimal | Low | HIGH | Low | Almost no jokes. Earn the weight. |
| **Climax** (Ch 28-29) | None | Character-only | DOMINANT | Minimal | Only character-voice humor survives |
| **Denouement** (Ch 30) | Medium | HIGH | Low | Medium | Humor returns as relief and warmth |

## The Cardinal Rules

1. **Never explain a joke.** If it needs explaining, rewrite it.
2. **The best jokes are true.** If you can't explain WHY it's funny, it probably isn't.
3. **Humor and emotion are not opposites.** The funniest moments and the most moving moments often share a sentence. Milo's cooking is funny. The party eating it in Volume 3 without complaint is devastating.
4. **Each character's humor is different.** Serox is funny because he's oblivious. Kalmaris is funny because he's dramatic. Longjohn is funny because he's blunt. Lamil is funny because he's silent. Lufnik is funny because he's formal. Milo is funny because he's precise. Don't mix their comedy styles.
5. **Earned darkness beats gratuitous darkness.** A joke that leads to a gut punch is more effective than unearned tragedy. Build them up to knock them down, then help them stand again.
